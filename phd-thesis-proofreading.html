Yes, you're almost certainly allowed. Within limits.

Most UK universities publish a proofreading policy, and most of them permit third-party proofreading inside defined boundaries. The principle is near-universal: a line between surface features, which a proofreader may correct, and content, structure and argument, which stay entirely yours.

What genuinely varies is the procedure around it. Whether you have to tell your supervisor. Whether a declaration is required at submission. Whether the proofreader needs approving in advance. Occasionally whether it's permitted at all on your particular programme.

Which is why the first thing I ask for isn't your thesis. It's a link to your university's policy. I'll read it properly, work inside it, and tell you if anything you've asked for falls the wrong side of the line.

And if your institution forbids it outright, I'll say so and turn the work down. A proofread that puts four years of your life at risk isn't worth £250 to you, and it certainly isn't worth £250 to me.

What I will do

  • Spelling, punctuation, and grammatical errors corrected.
  • Typographical errors, doubled words, and missing words.
  • Formatting and layout consistency: fonts, spacing, indentation, margins, heading levels.
  • Chapter, section, and subsection numbering checked as a sequence.
  • Cross-references verified – every "see Chapter 4" actually pointing at Chapter 4.
  • Figure and table numbering checked, and captions checked against the text that mentions them.
  • Contents page, list of figures, and list of tables cross-checked against the document itself.
  • Citation style applied consistently, and in-text citations cross-checked against the reference list in both directions.
  • Consistency of terminology, abbreviations, capitalisation, and number style throughout.
  • Appendices lettered or numbered in sequence and referenced correctly.

What I will not do

This list matters more than the one above, so it's spelled out.

  • Rewrite your sentences to sound better.
  • Reorder, merge or split paragraphs, sections or chapters.
  • Alter, sharpen or strengthen your argument.
  • Add or remove content of any kind.
  • Correct factual or subject-specific errors. Spot something that looks wrong and you'll get a comment, never a change.
  • Improve the substance of your analysis, literature review or discussion.
  • Write anything for you, anywhere in the thesis.

Everything comes back as tracked changes. If your supervisor, your examiner or your institution wants to see exactly what a proofreader did to your thesis, the answer is sitting in the file, itemised.

The things that actually break in a long document

A thesis isn't a very long essay. It's a Word document that's been restructured four times across three years, and it fails in ways short documents never do.

  • Cross-references that were correct until you moved a chapter, and have been quietly wrong ever since.
  • Contents pages generated automatically at some point, then hand-edited, and now agreeing with nothing at all.
  • Numbering sequences with a duplicate or a gap. Two 3.2s. No 4.7.
  • Citation drift. The first two chapters written to one style guide and the last two to a slightly hazier memory of it.
  • Terminology drift. A key concept that gains a hyphen in chapter three and loses it again in chapter six.
  • Heading styles applied by hand in some places and via Word styles in others, which stays invisible until the PDF gets generated.

None of this reflects on your research. All of it is exactly what a proofread exists for, and all of it is the sort of thing an examiner spots inside ten minutes.

Thesis proofreading questions

Am I allowed to pay someone to proofread my thesis?

At most UK universities, yes, within limits your institution sets out in its own proofreading policy. Nearly all of them draw the same line. Correcting surface features is permitted. Changing content, structure or argument is not.

What varies is the procedure. Whether you must tell your supervisor. Whether a declaration is required. Whether the proofreader needs approving first.

Send me your policy document before we start and I'll work inside it. If it forbids third-party proofreading, you'll be told, and I'll turn the work down.

What is a proofreader actually allowed to change?

Usually permitted: spelling, punctuation, grammar, typos, formatting and layout consistency, heading and numbering sequences, cross-references, figure and table numbering, contents page accuracy, citation style consistency.

Usually not permitted: rewriting sentences for style, restructuring paragraphs or chapters, altering the argument, adding or removing content, correcting subject-matter errors, improving the analysis.

Tracked changes throughout mean anybody who asks can see which side of that line every single edit falls on.

How much does it cost to proofread a PhD thesis?

£250 for up to 60,000 words, then £4 per additional 1,000. An 80,000-word thesis comes to £330. A 100,000-word thesis, £410. A 15,000-word Masters dissertation, £250.

Confirmed before you pay, and it doesn't move afterwards.

Do I have to declare that I used a proofreader?

Depends on the institution. Some want a declaration in the thesis or on a submission form. Some only want your supervisor to know. Some want nothing at all. Check your policy and your submission paperwork.

If a declaration is needed, I'll supply a written statement of what was done, describing the changes made and confirming that no content, structure or argument was altered.

How far in advance should I book?

Several weeks ahead, with the text reaching me two to three weeks before your deadline. Reading a full thesis takes time, and working through tracked changes on 90,000 words will take you longer than you're currently imagining.

Submission season is a genuine bottleneck. Reserving a date costs nothing. And if you're already tight on time, ask anyway. You'll get a straight yes or no that day.

Can you help with post-viva corrections?

Yes, and it costs less. Smaller, more focused work, quoted separately rather than charged at the full-thesis rate.

Send the examiners' correction list with the thesis. New text inserted into old chapters is where inconsistency appears. Different terminology, a different tense convention, occasionally a different citation style. That's the specific thing worth checking.

Will you check my references?

Within what proofreading permits, yes. Citation style applied consistently, in-text citations checked against the reference list and back the other way, entries formatted to one convention throughout.

What I won't do is verify that a source says what you claim it says. That's academic content rather than a surface feature, and it falls outside most university policies.

Do you proofread Masters and undergraduate dissertations too?

Yes, on exactly the same basis. Worth knowing that taught programmes often have stricter proofreading policies than doctoral ones, because the writing itself is being assessed more directly. So checking the policy matters more, not less.

Where a policy rules out third-party proofreading altogether, dissertation coaching at £50 a session is the legitimate route. I give feedback, you make every change yourself.
