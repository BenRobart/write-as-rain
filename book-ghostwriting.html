Why it hasn't happened yet

  • There are four beginnings in a folder, all written in the first fortnight of a January.
  • People have been telling you to write it down for years, and you've been agreeing with them for years.
  • You can talk about this for three hours without notes and can't get a page down.
  • Your father is ninety-one, and the stories only exist in one place.
  • Everyone in your industry has a book. You know more than most of them and have nothing to hand anybody.
  • You've done twenty thousand words and can't tell any more whether they're good, or in the right order, or the right twenty thousand.

How the work runs

  1. A conversation, and a straight answer

    Free, and no obligation attached. We talk for an hour about what you've got and what you want it to be. You'll get an honest verdict on whether there's a book in it, whether it's the book you think it is, and how long it would take. Sometimes the answer is that forty thousand words is the wrong container for this and a long article would serve you better. That answer is free too.

  2. The plan, and a chapter you can hold

    £450, and taken off the total if you go ahead. A chapter-by-chapter outline of the whole book, plus one real chapter written from a first interview. The point is that you see your own voice on a page before committing to several thousand pounds and half a year, rather than deciding on the strength of a description.

  3. The interviews

    An hour or two a fortnight, recorded, transcribed, and worked through in order of the plan rather than the order things happened. Fifteen to twenty-five hours in total for a full-length book. You don't prepare. Preparation produces the version you've told a hundred times, and the good material is nearly always underneath that one.

  4. Chapters, as they're written

    Each chapter comes to you when it's drafted, not all at the end. You correct facts, cut what you'd rather not say in public, and tell me where it doesn't sound like you. Corrections are expected rather than tolerated, and they're what stops a book drifting away from its subject over six months.

  5. The full draft, and what happens next

    The whole manuscript, revised, copyedited, and delivered in a form you can hand to an agent, a printer, or a self-publishing platform. Along with it: the recordings, the transcripts, and a plain account of your options, including the ones that don't involve paying anybody else.

The four books people arrive with

  • The business or expertise book. Twenty years of knowing something, currently distributed across your head, a few talks, and some slides. Usually 30,000 to 50,000 words, and its job is often to be handed to a prospective client rather than sold in a shop, which changes what a good one looks like.
  • The memoir for publication. A life with a shape to it, aimed at strangers. The hard part is never the remembering. It's deciding which fifteen years the book is actually about, and being willing to leave out the rest.
  • The family book. Thirty thousand words, printed a dozen times, given to grandchildren who'll read it properly in about fifteen years. Nobody's selling it, and it's still worth doing well. More on memoir and life story work here.
  • The novel. Possible, and it suits fewer people. Fiction needs a story that already exists in your head in some detail, because I can't interview you about events that never happened. A premise plus enthusiasm is a collaboration rather than a ghostwrite, and it's priced differently.

If the person whose story this is happens to be ill or very old, say so in your first message and we'll start with the recording. Everything else about a book can be done later. That part can't.

Sounding like you, on your best day

The target isn't a transcript. Nobody wants to read exactly how anybody talks, including you: real speech is full of restarts, and the third time you tell a story is usually the version worth keeping.

What carries over is the way you get to a point, the things you find funny, what you'd never say about yourself, and the words you reach for when you're not performing. Those come out of the recordings rather than out of a questionnaire, which is most of the argument for doing this by conversation.

The test I use on every chapter: would somebody who knows you well read this and hear you saying it? If not, it gets rewritten, and that judgement is yours rather than mine.

Credit, ownership, and money

Worth being blunt about, because this is where people are most unsure and least likely to ask.

  • You own it. The manuscript and the copyright are yours from the first draft. I take no rights, no royalties, no share of a deal, and no percentage of anything it earns. The fee is the whole of my interest in the book.
  • Your name goes on it. Sole credit is the default and the most common choice. "With" in smaller type, or "as told to", are both normal if you'd prefer them.
  • Nobody hears it from me. No credits, no portfolio, no client list, and no extracts used as samples. An NDA is fine if you'd rather have it in writing.
  • Staged payments. A quarter to begin, a quarter when the plan and opening chapters are approved, a quarter at the halfway mark, and a quarter on delivery. You're never a long way ahead of the work.
  • Stopping is allowed. If you stop at any stage you keep everything produced so far, recordings and transcripts included, and owe nothing beyond the stage you're in.

What I won't do

  • Invent things that didn't happen. Gaps in memory get handled honestly on the page, and there are good ways to do that. Filling them with something plausible is not one of them.
  • Write someone's life without them. If the subject is alive and able to talk, they take part. A book about a person, assembled from other people's accounts, is a different and much riskier undertaking.
  • Ghostwrite anything submitted for assessment. No dissertations, no theses, no coursework, whatever the deadline. That's the same answer the rest of this site gives, and it doesn't move for a bigger fee.
  • Promise you a publisher. Nobody can, and anyone who does is selling you something else.
  • Work for a share of royalties instead of a fee. It sounds generous and it puts us on opposite sides of every decision about the book.

Honest about publishing

A ghostwritten book is not a shortcut to an agent. Agents are entirely used to ghostwriting and don't hold it against anybody; what they weigh is whether the book is good and whether it will sell. A well-written manuscript improves your odds because it's well written, and for no other reason.

If you do want to submit it, the submission package is a separate £150: query letter, synopsis, and the opening chapters prepared properly.

Plenty of books never go near a publisher, on purpose. A business book handed to twelve of the right people has done its job, whatever its Amazon ranking says. A family book printed fifteen times for fifteen people has done its job completely. Both are perfectly good reasons to write one, and neither of them is a consolation prize.

How long, and what makes it slip

Three to four months for a book up to 30,000 words. Six to nine for a full-length one. That's first interview to finished manuscript.

What delays a book is hardly ever the writing. It's the fortnightly hour disappearing under something urgent, three times in a row, in a way that nobody quite notices until two months have gone. If your next quarter is genuinely impossible, starting in the one after that is a better decision than starting now and stalling in March.

If there's a fixed date on it – an anniversary, a birthday, a conference, somebody's health – put it in the first message and you'll get a straight answer about whether it's reachable.

Questions people ask

Will it actually be my book?

Yes, in the way that matters legally and the way that matters personally. You own the manuscript and the copyright outright, from the first draft onwards.

I take no royalties, no rights, no share of any deal, and no percentage of anything the book earns. The fee is the fee.

Every chapter is read and corrected by you as it's written, so nothing reaches the end unapproved – and the material was yours to begin with, because it came out of your mouth in the interviews.

Whose name goes on the cover?

Yours, unless you decide otherwise. Three arrangements are normal: your name alone with no mention of me anywhere, your name "with" mine in smaller type, or "as told to" for a life story where somebody else is narrating.

Sole credit is the most common and the default here. Whichever you pick, I don't list you as a client, don't use extracts as samples, and don't mention the book to anyone.

How much of my time will this take?

For a full-length book, expect fifteen to twenty-five hours of recorded conversation spread over several months, usually an hour or two a fortnight, plus reading time on each chapter as it lands. A shorter family book is nearer eight to twelve hours.

It's the one part nobody can do for you. Without your voice in the room there's nothing to write from, and the books that stall are almost always the ones where the interviews stopped happening.

How long does a book take?

Three to four months for a book up to 30,000 words, and six to nine months for a full-length one. That's from the first interview to a finished manuscript, and it assumes the conversations happen roughly on schedule.

What makes a book slip is almost never the writing. It's a diary that keeps eating the interview slot.

If you have a fixed date – an anniversary, a birthday, a conference, someone's health – say so at the start and I'll tell you honestly whether it's reachable.

When do I pay?

In four stages, so you're never far ahead of the work: a quarter to begin, a quarter when the chapter plan and opening chapters are approved, a quarter at the halfway mark, and the last quarter on delivery of the finished manuscript.

Before any of that, the £450 plan and sample chapter let you see real pages with your voice on them, and that £450 comes off the total if you go ahead.

What if I don't like the first chapters?

Then we stop, and that's precisely why the work is staged. You'll have seen a sample chapter before committing to the book at all, and the opening chapters before the second payment.

If the voice isn't right by then it isn't going to be, and paying for another five months of it would be a bad decision for both of us.

Stop at any stage and you keep everything produced up to that point – recordings, transcripts, the chapter plan, and every drafted chapter – with nothing further owed.

Can you get me a publisher or an agent?

No, and be wary of anybody who says they can. What I can do is make the manuscript as good as it can be and prepare the submission package – query letter, synopsis, and opening chapters – which is a separate £150 service.

Agents don't care whether a book was ghostwritten; it's ordinary in the trade. They care whether it's good and whether it sells.

Plenty of business books are never submitted anywhere, because their job is to be handed to a client rather than stocked in Waterstones. That's a perfectly sound reason to write one.

Can you ghostwrite a novel?

Yes, though it works differently from a memoir and suits fewer people. Fiction needs a story that already exists in your head in some detail – characters, the shape of the plot, how it ends – because I can't interview you about events that never happened.

If what you have is a premise and an enthusiasm, that's a collaboration rather than a ghostwrite, and it costs more because more of the invention lands on me.

Worth a conversation before either of us assumes which one it is.

Will anyone know?

Not from me. No credits, no portfolio, no client list, no extracts used as samples, and no mention of your book to anybody, including other clients. An NDA is fine if you'd rather have it written down.

Ghostwriting has been a normal part of publishing for about two centuries, and there's nothing about the arrangement that needs hiding. Whether anyone knows is your decision to make, not mine.
