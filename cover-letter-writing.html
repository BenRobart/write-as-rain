Careers · Letters & supporting statements

The letter is where you explain what your CV can't

A CV lists what you've done. It has no way of saying why this job, why you're leaving that one, what the two-year gap was, or why somebody from your industry is applying to theirs. The letter is the only place you get to speak, and most people spend its opening sentence announcing which job they're applying for. They know which job.

One of these is probably you

  • Forty applications sent with the same letter, changing only the company name. Once, not even that.
  • Your letter is your CV again, in sentences, which gives the reader nothing they didn't have.
  • It opens "I am writing to apply for the position of". You know that's weak. You don't know what goes there instead.
  • The advert wants a supporting statement of 1,500 words addressing the person specification, and you have no idea what that looks like.
  • There's a gap on the CV and you can't decide whether raising it makes it worse.
  • You're changing field, so the letter is carrying the entire argument, and it's buckling.
  • You suspect nobody reads them, and you're resentfully writing one anyway.

Does anybody actually read them?

Unevenly, and pretending otherwise would be silly. Some recruiters open the CV and never look at the letter at all. Others read the letter first and use it to decide whether the CV deserves their next two minutes.

What settles the question is the asymmetry. A good letter is sometimes ignored and never counts against you. Its absence, where one was asked for, is occasionally fatal, and it's read as either carelessness or a mail-merge.

There are three situations where the letter stops being optional and starts being the main event: a career change, an application with something on it that needs explaining, and any public sector job where the supporting statement is the document you're scored on.

The shape, in four paragraphs

Under a page. Three or four paragraphs, 300 to 400 words, and no second page ever.

  1. Why this job, specifically. Something true about them, connected to something true about you. No throat-clearing and no restating the advert.
  2. Your strongest piece of evidence, told properly. One example with a result attached beats four claims. This is the paragraph most letters skip in favour of adjectives.
  3. The thing the CV can't say. The pivot, the gap, the relocation, the step down, the reason you're leaving somewhere after eleven months. One clear sentence, unapologetic.
  4. What you want next. An interview. Say it plainly, without the phrase "I would relish the opportunity".

That's the whole architecture. Everything else in a cover letter is decoration, and decoration is what pushes it onto a second page.

Openings, before and after

The first sentence does more work than the rest of the letter put together, because it decides whether there is a rest of the letter.

  • Before: "I am writing to apply for the position of Operations Manager as advertised on Indeed."
    After: "You're opening a third site in October, which is the point at which most operations teams discover their processes only ever worked for two."
  • Before: "I am a passionate and highly motivated professional with excellent communication skills."
    After: "For six years I've handled the conversations nobody else wanted: the supplier who missed three deadlines, the customer who'd already given up on us."
  • Before: "Please find attached my CV for your consideration."
    After: Delete it. The CV is attached. They can see that.

The test is the same one that applies to a personal statement: could another applicant have written this sentence? If yes, it's doing no work at all.

Mirroring the advert without parroting it

The job advert is your brief. It tells you exactly which words this employer uses for the things they want, and which two or three requirements they've put first because they care most about them.

So the method is dull and effective. List the essential criteria in the advert's own order. Under each, write the strongest thing you've actually done that matches. Then build the letter from the top two or three, because a letter can carry three arguments and not eight.

What you're not doing is copying their sentences back at them. A letter that repeats the advert verbatim reads as flattery, or as somebody who has understood the exercise a little too literally. Use their vocabulary, your evidence.

Bear in mind where the letter is going. Pasted into a portal's plain text box, your bold vanishes, your bullets turn into stray hyphens, and occasionally your paragraph breaks disappear too. Any letter that only works as a beautifully formatted PDF is a letter that will sometimes arrive as a single grey block.

NHS and public sector supporting statements

A completely different document that happens to sit in the same slot, and treating it as a long cover letter is the most common reason strong candidates don't get shortlisted.

Shortlisting panels score your statement against the person specification. They award marks for evidence they can point to, and they cannot give you credit for something they had to infer. That single fact dictates everything about how the thing should be written.

  • Answer each essential criterion in turn, in the order the specification lists them. Headings are almost always welcome, and they make the panel's job easy, which is entirely the point.
  • One real example per criterion. Situation, what you did, what happened. Not "I have excellent communication skills", but the meeting, the problem, and what changed afterwards.
  • Cover the desirable criteria too, more briefly, where you can honestly claim them. They're often the tiebreak.
  • Match the length the advert asks for. If it says 1,500 words, 400 looks like you didn't bother and 3,000 looks like you can't follow instructions.
  • Show the values, don't quote them. Every NHS applicant mentions compassion. Very few describe a moment that demonstrates it.

Charity, university, and local government applications generally work the same way. If your advert names a person specification or a competency framework, that document is the brief, and the letter you were going to write is beside the point.

The awkward paragraphs

  • A gap. One sentence. Caring for a parent, illness now resolved, redundancy and retraining, time out with children. Unexplained holes make readers invent worse explanations than the truth.
  • Redundancy. Say it plainly. It happens to entire departments and carries no stigma unless your letter treats it as a confession.
  • Eleven months somewhere. Name it before they wonder. "The role changed shape six months in" is a complete and adequate answer.
  • A step down or a step sideways. Give the reason, because employers worry you'll leave the moment something better appears. If your reason is a genuine one, it will read as one.
  • Being overqualified. Address it directly and say what you want from this job. Silence here loses more offers than the seniority ever does.
  • Changing career entirely. This is the whole letter, not a paragraph in it, and it pairs with a CV rebuilt around the role you want.

Cover letter questions

Do employers still read cover letters?

Unevenly, and that's the honest answer. Some recruiters open the CV and never look at the letter. Others read the letter first and use it to decide whether the CV is worth their time. In the public sector the question doesn't arise, because the supporting statement is the document you're shortlisted on.

The asymmetry is what settles it: a good letter is sometimes ignored and never counts against you, while its absence, where one was requested, is occasionally fatal.

Write one whenever it's accepted, and keep it short enough to be read by someone who didn't intend to.

How long should a cover letter be?

Under one page. Three or four paragraphs, 300 to 400 words. A second page is read by nobody and signals that you couldn't decide what mattered.

Supporting statements are the exception and run to whatever the advert specifies, often 1,000 to 1,500 words, because they're doing a completely different job.

What should the first line say?

Something only you could have written about this particular job. Not "I am writing to apply for the position of Operations Manager as advertised on Indeed", which spends your best line telling them something they already know.

Start with the reason you're applying, stated concretely: the thing the organisation does that you want in on, or the specific overlap between their problem and your experience.

If your opening sentence would work for any other employer with the name swapped, it isn't earning its place.

Should I attach the letter or put it in the email?

If you're emailing an application, put the letter in the body of the email and attach the CV. Making somebody open a second attachment to read a covering note is a small friction with no upside.

If you're uploading through a portal, follow the field it gives you, and remember that pasting into a plain text box destroys your formatting: no bold, no fancy bullets, and sometimes no line breaks either. Write a version that survives that.

Who do I address it to?

A name, if you can find one in the advert, on the job page, or on LinkedIn, and "Dear Hiring Manager" if you genuinely can't. Never "To Whom It May Concern", which has the tone of a letter about a parking fine.

Don't spend an hour hunting: a wrong name is worse than a neutral greeting, and nobody has ever been rejected for "Dear Hiring Manager".

Should I mention a gap in my CV?

Yes, briefly, in the letter rather than the CV. One clear sentence closes it: caring for a relative, illness now resolved, redundancy followed by study, time out to raise children.

What creates suspicion is an unexplained two-year hole, because a reader who has to guess usually guesses worse than the truth.

Don't apologise and don't over-explain. State it, say what you did with the time if anything, and move on to the next paragraph.

What's a supporting statement, and how is it different from a cover letter?

It's the document NHS, local government, university, and charity applications ask for instead of a letter, and it's a different genre entirely.

Shortlisting panels score it against the person specification, criterion by criterion, and they can only award marks for what you've actually evidenced. So it works by addressing each essential criterion in turn, in the order the specification lists them, with a real example under each rather than a claim. Headings are usually welcome.

A charming, flowing letter that doesn't visibly answer criterion six will lose to a plainer statement that does.
