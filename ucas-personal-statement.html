The three questions, and how they change things

From the 2026 entry cycle, UCAS replaced the single free-text statement with three structured questions:

  1. Why do you want to study this course or subject?
  2. How have your qualifications and studies helped you to prepare for this course or subject?
  3. What else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why are these experiences useful?

The overall limit is still 4,000 characters across all three answers, with a minimum of 350 characters in each.

Formats and limits are set by UCAS and can change between cycles. Confirm the current requirements on the UCAS site before you write. I'll do the same before touching your draft.

This is a real change in strategy rather than a change in layout, and a lot of the advice still floating around online was written for the old format. That one rewarded a single graceful arc. This one doesn't. Each answer has to stand up on its own, and an elegant transition between them earns you precisely nothing.

Two things get harder. You can no longer hide a thin section inside a well-written whole, so a weak answer is now visibly a weak answer. And the temptation to repeat yourself across all three is enormous, which is expensive when 4,000 characters is all you've got.

One thing gets easier, though. You don't have to invent a structure any more. The questions are the structure. Most applicants find that a considerable relief, once they've finished mourning the essay.

Evidence, not adjectives

Nearly every weak statement fails in exactly the same way. It makes claims about the applicant instead of showing them.

  • "I am passionate about medicine." So is every other applicant, and not one of you has proved anything.
  • "I have excellent communication skills", demonstrated by a sentence that demonstrates nothing.
  • "This experience taught me the importance of teamwork", which every work experience placement in Britain apparently produces.

The fix is mechanical. Delete the claim and keep the evidence.

The specific thing you noticed on that ward. The argument in the book you disagreed with, and why you disagreed. The problem in the Saturday job that nobody had bothered to solve and you did. A tutor working through their four hundredth statement remembers detail. They stopped seeing adjectives some time in October.

That isn't a stylistic preference. I saw it in draft after draft when I was coaching Oxbridge applicants. Cut the claims and keep the specifics, and the applicant sounds more convincing for it.

One test, applied to every sentence: could a different applicant have written this exact sentence? If they could, it's doing no work at all, and it's eating characters you need somewhere else.

Why I won't write it for you

Partly principle. Mostly because it would be a rotten service. Three concrete reasons.

  1. Interviews. Medicine, dentistry, veterinary science, Oxbridge, and plenty of others interview directly from the statement. Being asked to expand on a paragraph you didn't write is a genuinely terrible five minutes, and it happens every year.
  2. Similarity detection. UCAS screens submitted statements against previous submissions and material online, then flags matches to the universities you applied to. Lifting phrasing from example statements isn't merely unoriginal. It's traceable.
  3. It reads as bought. Statements written by adults have adult cadences, adult vocabulary, and a suspicious absence of the specific slightly awkward detail that makes seventeen-year-olds convincing. Tutors read thousands a year. They know.

What happens instead: structural feedback first, then line editing on your own sentences, then a cut to the character limit that keeps the good bits. Your words the whole way through, sharper and better ordered, with the filler gone.

Beyond UCAS

The same approach applies to the applications that arrive after this one:

  • Oxbridge – where the subject-specific thinking matters more than the extracurriculars, and the statement is interview material.
  • Masters and PhD applications – a different genre entirely, closer to a research proposal with a personal element than to a UCAS statement.
  • US college essays – the Common App essay rewards a very different register: narrative, personal, and much less about academic preparation than British applicants expect.
  • Medicine and dentistry – where the reflective element carries far more weight than the list of placements, and "I want to help people" is the least useful sentence available to you.

Personal statement questions

Is it cheating to get help with my personal statement?

Feedback isn't cheating. Teachers and careers advisers hand it out as a matter of routine, and applicants at well-resourced schools have always had plenty. That's part of why the playing field was never level in the first place.

Having someone else write it is a different thing entirely, and you sign a declaration at submission saying you didn't. The line is authorship. If every sentence is yours and somebody has told you which ones are weak, you've had help. If the sentences aren't yours, you haven't had help. You've had a ghostwriter.

What are the three UCAS personal statement questions?

From 2026 entry, UCAS replaced the single statement with three:

  • Why do you want to study this course or subject?
  • How have your qualifications and studies helped you prepare for it?
  • What else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why is it useful?

4,000 characters total across all three, minimum 350 characters each. Check the UCAS site for your cycle's exact requirements before you write – they set the rules and they can change them.

How long should a UCAS personal statement be?

4,000 characters including spaces, across the three answers, with at least 350 in each. That's roughly 600–650 words in total.

Most people find it tight, which is the right problem to have. If you're struggling to reach the limit, the cause is nearly always that you're listing activities instead of reflecting on them – and reflection takes more words than listing does.

Can I use ChatGPT to write my personal statement?

Bad idea, and mostly not for the reason you're expecting.

AI-written statements have recognisable habits. A fondness for the word "delve". Tidy three-part lists. A confident closing paragraph that says nothing whatsoever. Admissions staff read thousands of these a cycle and they've learned the shape.

The real problem is different, though. An AI can't know the specific thing you noticed, or the book that changed your mind, or the moment on work experience that stayed with you afterwards. Those specifics are the only genuinely persuasive part of a personal statement.

Spellchecking with it, fine. Generating your reasons with it defeats the whole exercise.

Can I use the same statement for all five choices?

You have no choice – UCAS sends one set of answers to every course you apply for.

Which is exactly why applying for five noticeably different subjects causes trouble. A statement that convinces a law tutor you're committed to law won't also convince a physics tutor. If your choices are related, it's manageable. If they aren't, decide which subject the statement is really for and accept the others will read it as second place.

When should I start writing it?

July or August, the summer before you apply, so drafting doesn't collide with coursework and mocks.

Getting a first draft down early matters far more than getting it right early. Statements improve over several passes with gaps in between, and the gaps are what let you see your own writing clearly.

Check your cycle's UCAS deadlines – medicine, dentistry, veterinary science, Oxford and Cambridge all have a much earlier one than the main deadline.

What if I haven't done anything impressive?

Tutors aren't scoring the prestige of your activities. They're looking for evidence that you think seriously about the subject.

A Saturday job in a supermarket, described in terms of what it actually taught you about people or systems or pressure, beats an expensive overseas placement described as "life-changing" with no detail attached. Specificity and reflection are what count, and both are entirely available to applicants with no money and no contacts.

Does UCAS check statements for plagiarism?

Yes. Submitted statements are run through similarity detection against previous submissions and material available online, and matches are reported to the universities you've applied to.

So lifting phrasing from example statements you found online isn't merely unoriginal – it's traceable, and it lands on the desk of the people deciding about you.
