Prices · All of them, in public

Every price, and the sums behind them

There's no "contact us for pricing" anywhere on this site. Every number is on this page, the arithmetic is shown, and the quote you eventually get is the number you can work out here yourself in about ten seconds.

Manuscript editing runs on a base price covering the first 60,000 words, then a published rate per additional 1,000. Put your word count in and the calculator does the rest.

How the pricing works

One flat number for the job, agreed before any paid work starts, with no hourly meter running behind it. If the manuscript turns out to be harder than it looked, that's my problem and not your invoice.

The number comes from the word count of the file you send, which I check rather than take on trust. That check usually works in your favour, because most authors quote me the round number they've been telling people for months, and the file is a few thousand words short of it.

What can change a quote: a substantially rewritten manuscript arriving mid-edit. That's a different job, and you'd hear about it before I touched anything rather than on the invoice afterwards.

What's inside every price

  • Tracked changes on everything, so you accept or reject each edit individually.
  • Queries raised as comments rather than silent rewrites. If I don't know what you meant, I ask.
  • A style sheet with every copyedit, recording each decision so sequels and future edits stay consistent.
  • A full proofread as the final pass of every copyedit, not billed as a second service.
  • Questions after delivery answered free, by me, for as long as you have them.
  • A free sample edit first: your first 1,000 words, done properly, before any money is discussed.

Worth comparing rather than price: whether a quote includes a proofread, whether you get a style sheet, and whether the person editing your book is the person who'll answer your email six weeks later. Two quotes £150 apart often aren't for the same work at all.

What isn't inside it

  • Developmental editing doesn't include line editing or proofreading. Deliberately: your draft will change after the report, and polishing sentences you're about to delete wastes your money.
  • Formatting is separate. Editing gets the words right; formatting gets them onto a Kindle and a printed page. £75 and £150.
  • A second full pass after you've rewritten large parts is a new job. A look at the specific sections you changed usually isn't, and I don't charge for small ones.
  • Nothing academic gets written for you. Coaching and editing on your own work, always, and no exceptions on that one.

Why the numbers are public

Hidden pricing exists for one reason, which is that it lets the price depend on what you look like you can afford. An enquiry form that promises a bespoke quote is a negotiating position dressed as a service.

Published prices cost me the occasional job to somebody cheaper. They also mean nobody has to send an email to find out whether this is affordable, and nobody who does hire me wonders afterwards whether the person before them paid less.

The one thing I can't publish is a fixed price for work that has no unit to count. A ghostwritten life story assembled from twenty hours of recorded conversation isn't a per-word job, and pretending otherwise would produce a number that's wrong in one direction or the other. Those get quoted after a conversation, still as one flat figure, and still before you commit to anything.

Questions about money

How much does it cost to have a book edited in the UK?

Here, proofreading starts at £250, copyediting at £350, and developmental editing at £400. Each covers a manuscript up to 60,000 words, then £4, £6 and £7 per additional 1,000.

An 80,000-word copyedit is £470. A 120,000-word one is £710, with a full proofread included in both.

Across the UK market you'll see anything from £200 to well over £2,000 for the same length of book, which is why the sums matter more than the headline number.

Do you charge per word, per hour, or per project?

Per project, worked out from the word count.

Hourly billing puts you and me on opposite sides: every hour I spend costs you more, and you have no way of checking how long anything took. A flat price means a slow, difficult manuscript is my problem rather than your invoice.

It also means you know the number before you commit, which is the part most people actually want.

Is the quote actually fixed?

Yes. Once we've agreed a number it doesn't move, and there's no hourly meter running behind it.

The one thing that changes a quote is a change to the manuscript: if you send a substantially rewritten draft halfway through, that's a different job and I'll say so before doing anything.

The quote comes from the confirmed word count of the file you send, and I check that rather than take your estimate, which is usually to your benefit.

What if my document is much shorter than 60,000 words?

Then you shouldn't pay the base price, and you won't. A 4,000-word short story or a 6,000-word report is priced on its own terms, usually far below the manuscript minimums.

The base prices exist for book-length work. Send the document and you'll get a number that fits it.

Why are your prices lower than some editors and higher than others?

Below me you'll find services charging a few pounds per thousand words, usually offshore, often a light grammar pass by somebody who won't be there when you have a question. Above me are agencies with project managers, sales staff, and an office, all of which you pay for.

I'm one person with no overheads and no subcontractors, which lands where it lands.

What I'd compare instead of price: does the quote include a proofread, do you get a style sheet, and will the person editing your book be the person who answers your email in six weeks?

When do I pay?

Never before you've seen a sample edit of your own writing and a fixed number in writing. Nothing is taken to reserve a slot or to look at your work.

The timing of the invoice itself gets agreed when we agree the price, and for a long project, splitting it across the job is a normal request rather than an awkward one. Invoices are in pounds sterling.

Do you charge extra for a rush job?

No. There's no surcharge for a tight deadline, because either the date is possible or it isn't, and charging you more doesn't create hours in the week.

Tell me the deadline in your first message and you'll get a yes or no within a day. A no arrives early enough for you to find somebody else, which is worth more to you than a yes that turns into an apology in week five.

Is there a discount for editing a whole series?

There's no loyalty scheme, and I'd rather be straight about that than invent one.

What there is: the style sheet built on book one carries forward and grows, so later books need less reconstruction and fewer queries come back to you. If you're planning three books, say so early and we'll pencil the slots in, which protects your release schedule at no cost.

The price stays per book, worked out the same way each time.
