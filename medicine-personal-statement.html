Medicine and dentistry statements: the placements aren't the point

What you saw is on everybody's statement. What you made of it is on almost nobody's. The gap between those two sentences is where medicine applications are actually decided, and it's the thing four weeks of rewriting rarely closes on its own.

Where medicine applicants get stuck

  • You have the grades, the admissions test score, and the placements, and the draft still reads like a CV in sentences.
  • "I have always wanted to help people" is your opening line, you know it's weak, and nothing better has arrived.
  • You shadowed a consultant for a week and can't say anything about it beyond that it was interesting.
  • Your school's guidance was written for the old single-statement format and nobody has updated it.
  • You've read nine example statements and now everything you write sounds like all nine.
  • Four medicine choices, one backup in something else, and one statement expected to satisfy both.
  • Your deadline is in October while everyone around you is relaxed until January.

What they're reading for

Not enthusiasm. Every applicant is enthusiastic, and enthusiasm is the cheapest thing on the page.

Admissions staff are looking for evidence that you understand the job you're applying for, including the parts nobody puts in a prospectus: chronic illness that doesn't resolve, patients who don't follow advice, the paperwork, the rota, the colleague you'd rather not be on shift with. An applicant who writes about the unglamorous middle of medicine with clear eyes is making a far stronger case than one describing a dramatic resuscitation they watched from a corner.

Worth knowing, and worth checking rather than assuming: schools use the statement very differently. Some score it during shortlisting. Some barely read it before interview. Several use it as interview material, which is the highest-stakes use of all, because you'll be asked to expand on a sentence you wrote in August. Look at the admissions pages for your four choices before deciding how much weight to give each paragraph.

Reflection, shown rather than defined

Everyone tells applicants to reflect. Almost nobody says what that looks like on the page, so here are the same three days of work experience written twice.

Described: "I spent a week shadowing a respiratory consultant, where I observed ward rounds and outpatient clinics. This taught me the importance of teamwork and communication in a multidisciplinary setting."

Reflected: "In one fifteen-minute clinic appointment, the consultant spent eleven of those minutes on a patient's housing. The flat was damp, the asthma was uncontrollable while it stayed damp, and no inhaler was going to fix a wall. I'd assumed medicine was mostly diagnosis. Most of that afternoon was arranging things doctors have no direct power over, and getting nowhere politely."

The second version is barely longer. It also does something the first can't: it proves you were actually in the room, and it gives an interviewer something to ask about that you can answer for ten minutes without preparation.

The test is mechanical. Cover the placement and read what's left. If the remaining sentences would fit any placement anywhere, you've described rather than reflected, and those characters are being spent on nothing.

One rule applied to every sentence: could a different applicant have written this exact sentence? If they could, it's doing no work, and in a statement with a hard character limit, work is the only reason a sentence gets to stay.

Work experience, and the fairness of it

The most useful thing anyone told me while I was working with applicants: the placement almost never matters, and the two sentences after it always do.

A year of Saturdays in a care home demonstrates more about your understanding of care than five days shadowing a surgeon, and a good many medical schools now say so openly. Sustained beats prestigious. Care work, volunteering, paid healthcare roles, ambulance service volunteering, and jobs involving frightened or difficult members of the public all count as real evidence.

If you couldn't get anything, say so plainly and demonstrate understanding by another route: an online observational programme, a conversation with a relative who works in the NHS written up honestly, a part-time job with genuine responsibility. Applicants who admit access was difficult and then show real insight consistently read better than applicants who inflate one afternoon into a calling.

The three questions, applied to medicine

From the 2026 entry cycle the single statement was replaced by three structured questions, and the strategy for medicine changes with it.

  1. Why this course? The trap here is the origin story. A grandparent's illness is true, common, and by itself proves nothing. What proves something is the thinking you've done since.
  2. How have your studies prepared you? Not a list of subjects. The specific thing chemistry taught you about accepting evidence you didn't like, or the EPQ that changed your mind halfway through.
  3. What else, outside education? Where the work experience, volunteering, and part-time jobs live, along with the sport or music that shows you can absorb pressure over years rather than describe absorbing it.

Formats and character limits are set by UCAS and can change between cycles. Confirm the current requirements on the UCAS site before you write, and I'll do the same before touching your draft. The general UCAS page covers the format in more detail.

Dentistry is not medicine with teeth

The most common dentistry statement I see is a medicine statement with one noun changed, and admissions tutors spot it instantly, because they've spent years reading applications from people whose first choice was something else.

  • Manual dexterity is a real criterion. Evidence it: an instrument, sewing, model making, art, anything requiring hands that do what they're told. This belongs in the statement rather than being saved for interview.
  • Most dentistry happens in a small business. Understanding that practices are run as businesses, with the pressures that implies, marks out an applicant who has actually looked.
  • Relationships run for decades. Not one admission, one discharge. That changes what good communication means.
  • A large part of the job is fear. Working with people who genuinely do not want to be in the chair. If you've seen that handled well, write about it.
  • Answer "why dentistry rather than medicine" on the page. They're wondering. Leaving it unanswered is worse than any answer you could give.

The October deadline changes your calendar

Medicine, dentistry, and veterinary courses share an earlier UCAS deadline in October, alongside Oxford and Cambridge, rather than the main January one. Check the exact date for your cycle, because UCAS sets it and it moves.

The practical consequence is a collision. Your statement is being written in the same weeks as admissions test preparation and the start of Year 13, which is why the drafting window that works is July and August. A first draft in July that you dislike is worth considerably more than a perfect plan in September.

If you're reading this in late September with nothing written, that's still workable and it's a very common position. Say so in your first message and we'll start from where you actually are.

Everything in it is interview material

Medicine interviews, in whatever format your schools use, draw directly on what you wrote. That makes the statement a promise you'll have to keep in a room some months later.

So a claim like "I'm interested in medical ethics" is an invitation. Somebody will ask which case, and what you concluded, and why. If the honest answer is that you read one article, either read three more or take the sentence out.

Part of the edit is exactly this: marking every sentence that writes a cheque your interview would have to cash, and asking whether you can cash it. Some applicants take that as a prompt to go and do the reading, which is the ideal outcome. Others cut the line, which is the next best.

Why it has to be your writing

Every applications page on this site says the statement won't be written for you. For medicine the reasons go further than principle.

  1. You will be interviewed on it. Being asked to expand on a paragraph an adult wrote for you is a genuinely bad five minutes, and it happens every cycle.
  2. UCAS runs similarity detection against previous submissions and material online, and reports matches to the universities you've applied to.
  3. Probity starts now. Medical schools treat honesty as a professional attribute from the application onwards, not from graduation. A bought statement is a fitness-to-practise question in disguise, and it's a poor way to begin a career built on people believing what you tell them.

What happens instead: structural feedback first, then line editing on your own sentences, then the cut to the character limit that keeps what's doing work. Your words throughout, in a better order, with the filler gone.

Medicine & dentistry questions

How do I make a medicine personal statement stand out?

Not with better adjectives, and not with a more dramatic opening.

What separates statements is reflection: the specific thing you noticed, what it changed in your understanding of the job, and what you did about it afterwards. Nearly every weak statement describes placements. Nearly every strong one interrogates one or two of them and lets the rest go.

Admissions staff are reading hundreds of applications from people with the same grades and similar experience. The only variable left is what you made of it.

Do medical schools actually read the personal statement?

It varies by school, and this is worth checking rather than assuming. Some score it formally as part of shortlisting. Some barely look at it before interview and rely on the admissions test and academic record. Several don't score it at all but hand it to interviewers, which is arguably the highest-stakes use of the three, because you will be asked to expand on something in it.

Look at the admissions pages for your four choices. They generally say, and it should change how much effort each sentence has to justify.

How much work experience do I need for medicine?

Less than the forums will tell you, and of a different kind.

Sustained caring experience beats a glamorous week: a Saturday job in a care home for a year says more about your understanding of the work than five days shadowing a cardiothoracic surgeon. Many medical schools now say explicitly that they value care work, volunteering, and paid healthcare roles alongside or above hospital shadowing, partly because shadowing depends so heavily on who your parents know.

What matters in the statement is never the placement itself. It's the two sentences after it.

What if I couldn't get any work experience?

It's a common position and it isn't fatal.

Volunteering of any kind involving people, a part-time job with real responsibility, online observational programmes run by universities and royal colleges, and honest engagement with what the work involves all count. So does a conversation with a relative who is a nurse or a paramedic, written up properly.

What doesn't work is pretending. An applicant who says plainly that access was difficult, and then demonstrates real understanding of the job anyway, reads far better than one who inflates a single afternoon into a vocation.

Is the deadline for medicine different?

Yes. Medicine, dentistry, and veterinary courses share an earlier UCAS deadline in October, along with Oxford and Cambridge, rather than the main deadline in January. Confirm the exact date for your cycle on the UCAS site, since they set it and it can move.

The practical consequence is that your statement is being written in the same weeks as your admissions test preparation and the start of Year 13, which is why the sensible drafting window is July and August.

Can I apply for medicine and another subject with the same statement?

You can apply, since UCAS sends one set of answers to every choice, but you can't serve both well.

A statement built to convince a medical school will read to a biomedical sciences tutor as somebody who wants to be somewhere else, and a statement hedged across both convinces nobody.

The usual approach is to write it for medicine and accept that your fifth choice reads it as a second preference, which admissions staff are entirely used to. Some universities suggest contacting them directly about a backup choice. It's worth asking them rather than guessing.

Will you write my personal statement for me?

No, and for medicine the reasons are sharper than usual.

You will be interviewed, often through several stations, and asked to expand on things you wrote. UCAS runs submitted statements through similarity detection and reports matches to the universities you applied to. And medical schools treat honesty as a professional attribute from the first day of the application, not from graduation. A bought statement is a probity question wearing a disguise.

What you get instead is structural feedback, line editing on your own sentences, and a cut to the character limit that keeps the parts doing real work.

What's different about a dentistry personal statement?

More than most applicants expect, and the commonest failure is a medicine statement with the word swapped.

Dentistry wants evidence of manual dexterity, which is a genuine selection criterion, and it can come from anywhere: playing an instrument, sewing, model making, hairdressing. It wants an understanding that most dentistry is done in a small business rather than a hospital, that patients are seen over decades rather than for one admission, and that a large part of the job is managing fear in people who don't want to be there.

A statement that shows you know why dentistry rather than medicine is doing something no amount of enthusiasm can replace.
