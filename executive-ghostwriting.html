The posts you keep meaning to write

You know what you think. You've said it in meetings, on calls, and to whoever was standing next to you at the conference. It has never once made it onto LinkedIn.

One conversation a month, and a month of posts comes back written the way you actually talk. Nothing goes out without your approval, and nobody knows I exist.

Get in touch

Why it never happens

  • You open the box, type three lines, decide they sound obvious, and close it again.
  • You posted twice in a fortnight last spring, then nothing until somebody asked whether you'd left the company.
  • A competitor with worse opinions posts every Tuesday, and the enquiries go to them.
  • There's something good in your drafts. It's been there since February because the ending doesn't land.
  • Somebody told you to build a personal brand, which is possibly the least appealing sentence in business English.
  • The sharpest thing you said this month was in a Slack message, to one person, who replied with a thumbs up.

How the month runs

  1. One conversation

    Thirty to forty minutes, recorded, whenever suits. Not an interview about your brand. What irritated you this month, what a client got wrong, what you've changed your mind about since last year, the thing you say to every new hire in week one. Most of a month's material arrives sideways, in something you mention and then move past.

  2. Drafts, in a batch

    Written in your voice and sent together, so you can read them in one sitting rather than being interrupted eight times. Each one leads with the opinion, because LinkedIn shows roughly two lines before the "see more" link, and a wind-up costs you the whole post.

  3. Your sign-off

    Change anything, cut anything, kill anything, with no explanation owed. Nothing is published without your approval. You post them yourself, which takes a minute and means I never need your password.

  4. Next month, better

    What landed and what sank feeds the following batch. By month three the drafts need noticeably less editing, because I've learned where your opinions actually sit rather than where I assumed they did.

Finding the voice

Voice isn't vocabulary, which is why the copy-my-tone approach produces something that reads like a stranger doing an impression. It lives in what you notice, what you refuse to say, and how long you're willing to take getting to the point.

Three things make it work:

  • The recording. Not for transcription. For the phrases you reach for without thinking, the places you hedge, and the ones where you don't hedge at all.
  • What you've already written. Old posts, a few emails, anything published under your name. Ten of your emails tell me more than an hour of you describing your tone.
  • The never list. Written down in month one and added to forever. Words you'd be embarrassed to be caught using. Mine starts with "journey", "excited to announce", and "thrilled", and yours will have its own private entries.

Before any money changes hands: one conversation, and one post written from it, free. If it doesn't sound like you, that's the end of it and we part as friends. It's the only honest way to sell this, because the whole service rests on a thing you can't judge from a description.

What gets written

The test for any post: could somebody else in your industry have written it? If they could, it isn't yours, and it isn't worth the slot.

  • Before: "Delighted to share that the team has been working hard on some exciting developments in the compliance space."
    After: "We turned down two clients last quarter. Both would have paid on time. Here's the rule we used, and what it cost us."
  • Before: Five tips on hiring, all of them true, none of them arguable.
    After: The one thing you do differently from everybody else who hires for this role, and the expensive mistake that taught you to.
  • Before: "What are your thoughts?" bolted onto the end.
    After: Nothing bolted onto the end. A post worth arguing with gets argued with.

Specifics do the work. A number, a date, a decision that went badly, the actual sentence a client said on the call. Generalities are what people scroll past, and they're also what everybody defaults to when they write their own posts at eleven at night.

Is this dishonest?

Worth answering properly rather than hurrying past, because most people wonder and few ask.

The opinions are yours. The examples are yours. The judgement about what to say in public is yours, and you approve every word before it exists anywhere. What you're buying is an hour of writing you don't have, and the craft of getting a thought into a hundred and fifty words that hold together.

Annual reports, ministerial speeches, keynote addresses, and a fair number of the books on your shelf were made this way. The arrangement is older than LinkedIn by about two centuries.

Where the line sits, and it doesn't move:

  • No invented anecdotes. If it didn't happen, it doesn't go in, however well it would land.
  • No results you didn't get. Numbers come from you and go out as you gave them.
  • No positioning you as an expert in something you aren't. That's fiction with your name on it, and it gets found out in the first conversation it wins you.

If you'd rather say publicly that you work with a writer, that's entirely your call, and it changes nothing about how the work is done.

What nobody can promise

No engagement guarantees. Anybody offering you a number is either buying it or making it up, and both show.

No pods, no bought engagement, no comment rings. The people you want to reach can spot a post with forty generic comments and no real conversation, and the reach it buys is worth less than the credibility it costs.

What posting properly for six months does tend to produce is inbound from people who already half-trust you by the time they get in touch. It also tends to produce very little for the first two months. Quitting in week seven is the most common way this fails, and it fails quietly, which is why so many people do it twice.

The numbers worth watching aren't likes. Saves and shares mean somebody wanted it later or wanted somebody else to see it, and a single reply from the right person beats four hundred impressions from the wrong ones.

The ghost stays ghostly

No credits, no portfolio, no case studies, and no client list anywhere on this site. Your name doesn't appear here, and I don't mention who I write for, including to other clients.

An NDA is fine if your legal team would prefer the arrangement written down. Nothing about the work changes; it just gets signed.

Inside your own company it's your call entirely. Some clients bring a marketing colleague onto the monthly call. Others have never mentioned it to anybody, which is also completely normal.

Who this suits, and who it doesn't

It suits founders, consultants, fractional executives, and anybody whose next client will read their profile before the first call. If your reputation arrives in the room before you do, this is the work that builds it.

It doesn't suit three groups, and saying so up front saves everybody a call:

  • Anybody who wants volume for its own sake. Thirty scheduled posts a month is a social media agency's job, and there are good ones.
  • Anybody who wants their account run for them. I write; you post. That line doesn't move.
  • Anybody who can't give up forty minutes a month. Without the conversation there's no raw material, and what comes back is the same interchangeable filler as everyone else's feed. Silence is better than that.

Questions people ask

Isn't ghostwriting dishonest?

The opinions are yours, the examples are yours, the judgement is yours, and you approve every word before it goes anywhere. What you're buying is the hour of writing you don't have.

Company reports, ministerial speeches, and a fair share of the books on your shelf were made the same way. It's a long-standing arrangement rather than a modern trick.

The line I hold: no invented anecdotes, no results you didn't get, and no positioning you as an expert in something you aren't.

Will it actually sound like me?

That's the whole job, and it's why the month starts with a conversation instead of a questionnaire. Voice lives in what you notice, what you refuse to say, and how quickly you get to the point.

I also read what you've already written – old posts, a few emails, anything published – because ten of your emails say more about how you sound than an hour of describing it.

The first post is free precisely so you can check before any money changes hands.

How much of my time does this take?

Thirty to forty minutes on a call once a month, plus the time it takes to read the drafts. Call it an hour.

If you can't spare that, this won't work and I'd rather say so now: without the conversation there's no raw material, and what comes back is the generic filler everybody scrolls past.

What if I hate a draft?

Say so and it's rewritten, with no explanation owed and no charge.

Some months one lands badly, usually because I've misread how strongly you hold an opinion. That's useful information rather than an awkward moment, and it makes the next batch better. Nothing is ever published without your approval.

Do you need access to my LinkedIn account?

No, and I'd rather not have it. Drafts are delivered to you and you post them yourself, which takes about a minute and keeps every credential where it belongs.

It also means the account is unambiguously yours, and there's no scenario where somebody else is typing under your name in real time.

Can you guarantee engagement or leads?

No, and anybody guaranteeing you a number is either buying it or inventing it. No pods, no bought engagement, no comment rings – they're visible to exactly the people you want to reach.

What posting properly for six months tends to produce is inbound conversations from people who already trust you. It also tends to produce very little for the first couple of months, and giving up in week seven is the most common way this fails.

What's included in the retainer?

The monthly conversation either way, then the writing. £300 a month buys four LinkedIn posts; £500 buys eight, or four posts and one email newsletter, decided month by month depending on what you've got to say. Two rounds of edits on each piece, and a rewrite when something misses.

Longer work – a talk abstract, a case study, an opinion piece for a trade publication – is quoted separately rather than squeezed into the retainer.

Is there a minimum term?

No. It runs month to month and you can stop at the end of any month.

Will anyone know?

Not from me. No credits, no portfolio, no case studies, and no client list on this website – your name never appears here. I'll sign an NDA if your legal team would prefer one.
