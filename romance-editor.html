You've probably had at least one of these

  • An editor "fixed" a love scene into something you didn't recognise, and you spent an entire evening clicking Reject.
  • A reviewer found the continuity error before anyone on your team did. His sister's age. The name of the bar.
  • You publish to a schedule, and "six to eight weeks, possibly longer" doesn't qualify as one.
  • Someone called your third-act breakup contrived, and you realised halfway through their notes that they don't read romance.
  • You're writing dual POV and you honestly can't tell any more whether the two voices are different.
  • Your ARC team found typos. In a manuscript you'd already paid somebody to catch typos in.

The series bible does most of the work

Romance readers pay closer attention than any readership I've come across. Your hero's brother was twenty-six in book one. Somebody will remember that. Somebody will also remember the coffee shop was on Mill Street, not Mill Road, and that your heroine was allergic to shellfish in a scene you wrote three years ago and have entirely forgotten writing.

And they will mention it. In a review. With the chapter number.

So the style sheet I build on book one isn't an afterthought, it's the actual asset. It holds every character name, nickname and surname spelling, plus who calls whom what. It holds the physical details that get contradicted first, which are almost always eye colour and height. Ages and birthdays. The running timeline, including how long ago the events of the previous book happened. Street layouts and who lives where. Your punctuation decisions, down to whether text messages are italicised.

Then it travels with the series and grows. By book four it's earning its keep. By book six it's the reason nobody notices anything is wrong, which is the entire ambition.

Your heat level is yours

Closed door, open door, or thoroughly open door. That's a decision you made about your book and your readers, and revisiting it isn't an editor's job. I edit an explicit scene exactly the way I'd edit a car chase, looking at clarity, rhythm, continuity, and whether the choreography survives contact with a reader who is picturing it.

What I will flag: a hand in two places at once. A sudden lurch into clinical vocabulary that breaks the mood. An anatomical impossibility. A consent beat that reads differently on the page than you meant it to.

Those are craft notes and they arrive as queries in the margin, never as corrections.

The only test that matters here: would a reader who bought this book for exactly what the cover promised be pulled out of the scene? If not, it stays as written. My own comfort level isn't a variable in that equation, and if it were I'd be editing the wrong genre.

Trope literacy, so nothing gets "corrected"

Enemies to lovers. Grumpy/sunshine. Fake dating, second chance, only one bed, forced proximity, marriage of convenience. You haven't accidentally fallen into these. They're the promise on the cover, and your readers turned up for the execution.

An editor from outside romance will query the black moment as unmotivated, call the grovel excessive, and ask whether the epilogue is really necessary. It is. It's load-bearing. My notes are about whether your version of a beat earns itself, which is a completely different question from whether the beat belongs.

Plenty of things genuinely are worth raising, mind you. An HEA that lands before the central conflict has resolved. A dark moment caused by a misunderstanding one sentence of dialogue would fix. A hero who needs another twenty pages before a reader will forgive him for chapter four.

Dual POV that actually sounds like two people

Alternating first person is the default across huge swathes of the market, and it's much harder than it looks. By chapter twenty both narrators have usually drifted towards the same rhythms and the same handful of verbal tics, because they're both being written by one person on the same wet afternoons.

I read for it deliberately. Vocabulary range, sentence construction, what each character notices first walking into a room, and (more revealing) what they never comment on at all. Where the voices have converged I'll show you the specific paragraphs rather than leaving a note saying "differentiate these more", which has never once helped anybody.

Release schedules

Rapid release only works when every link in the chain is predictable, and editing is usually the link that isn't. So tell me your publication date before you tell me your word count.

If the date can't be done, you'll know within a day. I'd rather turn work down than take a booking and deliver in week nine. Missing a release date costs you the preorder, the newsletter momentum and the also-boughts, which between them are worth a great deal more than an edit. Planning a series? Book the slots ahead and the schedule holds.

What comes back to you

  • The full manuscript in Word, tracked changes throughout, every edit accepted or rejected individually.
  • Comments wherever I'm unsure. Questions, never silent rewrites.
  • Your style sheet as a separate document. Yours to keep, reuse, and hand to your next narrator.
  • A full proofread as the final pass, included in the copyedit.

Romance editing questions

How much does it cost to edit a romance novel?

Copyediting starts at £350 for up to 60,000 words, then £6 per additional 1,000. In practice that means:

  • 55,000-word category romance, £350
  • 80,000-word contemporary, £470
  • 110,000-word romantasy, £650

A full proofread is baked into the copyedit, so that stage isn't billed twice. Proofreading on its own starts at £250.

Will you tone down the spice?

No. Your heat level is an authorial decision and a marketing decision, and it isn't mine to make.

I'll flag a body part in two places at once, or a sudden lurch into clinical vocabulary that kills the mood, because those are continuity and register problems. What I won't do is soften your language because it made me uncomfortable. If that were a factor I'd be editing cookbooks.

Can you edit a whole series and keep it consistent?

Yes, and it's the strongest argument for sticking with one editor across a series rather than whoever happens to be cheapest that month.

The style sheet from book one carries forward and grows as it goes. That's what catches the brother who somehow gains two years between instalments, the bar that changes street, and the surname whose spelling drifted somewhere around book three and never came back.

How fast can you turn a book around on a rapid release schedule?

Give me the publication date first and the word count second. You'll have a yes or no within a day.

If it's a no, you'll get that straight away rather than in week five when it's too late to find anyone else. For a planned series, pencilling slots in months ahead is what actually keeps a schedule intact, and it costs nothing to do.

Do I need a copyedit or just a proofread?

If beta readers are the only people who've seen it, you want a copyedit. Beta readers tell you whether they enjoyed the book. They don't catch tense slips, five chapters opening with the same construction, or a timeline that quietly breaks in chapter twelve.

Proofreading assumes a copyedit already happened. Book one for a manuscript that clearly hasn't had it and I'll tell you before taking payment. The free sample usually makes it obvious to both of us within a page.

Do you edit dark romance and morally grey heroes?

Yes, and without editorial commentary on your choices. Dark romance has its own conventions and a readership that knows them intimately. An editor who treats the subgenre as a problem to be managed is the wrong editor for it.

Craft questions still get raised, obviously. Whether a consent beat reads on the page the way you intended it to. Whether the grovel is proportionate to what he actually did. But they go in the margin as queries, for you to answer or ignore.

Can you edit a book that started as fanfiction?

Yes. A good many published romances did exactly that, so it's normal work rather than a confession.

The usual job is filing off the last of the serial numbers. Shorthand that only lands if you know the source material. Characters who were never physically described, because everybody already knew what they looked like. Pacing built for weekly chapter drops rather than one long sitting. I'll flag anything still reading as residue from the original.

Should I use British or American spelling?

Follow the setting and the market, not your own passport. A romance set in Boston with British spellings and single quotation marks reads as very slightly off to exactly the readers most likely to buy it. Works the other way round too.

Whichever we settle on goes on the style sheet and gets applied everywhere, including the bits people forget about. Date formats. Whether punctuation sits inside or outside the quotation marks. Whether it's "Mr" or "Mr."
