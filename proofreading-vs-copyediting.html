Guide · Which edit you need

Proofreading, copyediting, developmental editing: what each one changes

Three different jobs, done at three different points in a book's life. The names tell you almost nothing about which one you want, and the industry uses them inconsistently, so this page shows you the same paragraph edited three ways instead of defining terms at you.

Why nobody knows which one to book

  • Two editors quoted you for "editing". The quotes were £400 apart and neither said what the difference was.
  • Someone told you the manuscript needs a line edit, which is a fourth word nobody has defined either.
  • You paid for a proofread and got back two hundred comments about character motivation.
  • Or the reverse: a clean file came back, and reviewers still say the middle drags.
  • Your beta readers loved it, which tells you the story works and tells you nothing about the sentences.
  • Every website you've read defines the terms slightly differently, and you're beginning to suspect they're making it up.

The one-line version

Before the worked example, the shortest honest summary I can give you:

  • Developmental editing looks at the book. Structure, pacing, character, point of view, whether the thing works at all.
  • Copyediting looks at the sentences. Word choice, clarity, consistency, and the eye colour that changed in chapter nine.
  • Proofreading looks at the page. Typos, punctuation, page numbers, the running head still claiming you're in chapter four.

Or shorter still: developmental editing changes what you wrote. Copyediting changes how you wrote it. Proofreading is the last person in the room before the door closes.

That's the theory. The theory has never once helped anybody decide, which is why the rest of this page exists.

The same paragraph, three ways

Here's a paragraph of the sort that turns up in a second draft. It isn't a disaster and it isn't finished, which makes it a useful specimen. Watch what each level of editing does to it, and more importantly, what each level deliberately leaves alone.

As it arrives

Sarah walked into the room and looked around the room. She was nervous, she had never been to a party like this before and she didn't know anyone their. It was a big room with alot of people in it and the music was very loud. She thought about leaving. Then she saw Tom by the window and felt relieved.

After a proofread

Sarah walked into the room and looked around the room. She was nervous. She had never been to a party like this before and she didn't know anyone there. It was a big room with a lot of people in it and the music was very loud. She thought about leaving. Then she saw Tom by the window and felt relieved.

Three fixes: "their" to "there", "alot" to "a lot", and the comma splice broken into two sentences. Everything else is untouched, on purpose. The paragraph still says "room" three times in two sentences and still tells you Sarah is nervous rather than showing you. Both are real problems, and neither is a proofreader's business.

After a copyedit

Sarah stopped just inside the door. She had never been to a party like this, and she didn't know a soul in the place. The music was loud enough to feel in her sternum. She was working out how to leave without being noticed when she saw Tom by the window.

The repetition goes. "She was nervous" goes, because the next three sentences were already doing that work and the label was undercutting them. "Felt relieved" goes for the same reason. The rhythm gets a short sentence to land on. In the margin, two comments: one asking whether "sternum" is her vocabulary or mine, and one flagging that she went to Marcus's parties twice in chapter two, so "never been to a party like this" may need a qualifier.

After a developmental edit

Sarah walked into the room and looked around the room. She was nervous, she had never been to a party like this before and she didn't know anyone their. It was a big room with alot of people in it and the music was very loud. She thought about leaving. Then she saw Tom by the window and felt relieved.

Identical. Not one word touched, and that's the service working correctly. What you get instead is a comment: this is the third scene where Sarah arrives somewhere anxious and is settled by the sight of Tom. The beat is doing the same job each time, so by chapter eleven the reader is ahead of her and slightly impatient. Consider whether she can walk in already decided here, and let Tom be the complication rather than the reassurance. If you take that note, the paragraph gets rewritten anyway. Which is precisely why nobody polished it first.

The useful thing to notice: the developmental edit changed nothing on the page and did the most work. The proofread changed three things and did the least. And the copyedit is the only one of the three that both improved the writing and left it correct, which is why it's what most people actually want when they ask for "an edit".

How to tell where your draft actually is

Four questions, answered honestly, get most people to the right answer in about a minute.

  1. Could you write a one-sentence summary of what the book is about, without hedging? If that sentence keeps growing extra clauses, the structure isn't settled and you want developmental work.
  2. Has anyone who isn't fond of you read the whole thing? If not, you don't yet know whether the story works, and no amount of sentence polish will tell you.
  3. Are you still moving scenes around? Then a copyedit is premature. You'd be paying for sentences you're about to delete.
  4. Has the manuscript already been properly copyedited by somebody? If no, a standalone proofread is the wrong purchase, however tempting the price is.

Most manuscripts that arrive here want a copyedit. Some want a developmental read first, and a few want somebody to say the thing is basically fine and to stop poking it. That last answer is free and I give it more often than you'd think.

The words the industry can't agree on

Part of your confusion isn't confusion. Editors genuinely use these terms differently, and nobody polices it. So here's what I mean by each, and what you should ask any other editor to confirm before you compare prices.

  • Line editing. Sentence-level craft: rhythm, imagery, voice, the paragraph that drags. Some editors sell it as a separate stage between developmental and copyediting. I don't, because it means reading your book twice and billing twice. It's inside what I call copyediting.
  • Structural editing. The same thing as developmental editing, more or less, with the emphasis on the shape rather than the characters. Different traditions, one job.
  • Manuscript assessment or critique. A written report with no marks on the manuscript at all. Cheaper than a developmental edit and genuinely useful when you want a verdict rather than a plan.
  • Beta reading. Readers, not editors. They tell you where they got bored, which is information no professional can give you, because I can't un-know how books work.
  • Sensitivity or authenticity reading. A reader with lived experience of something in your book, checking how it lands. Not editing, and not something I offer or pretend to.
  • Proof-edit. Usually means a light copyedit sold at proofreading prices. Ask what's actually included.

The order matters, and getting it wrong costs money

Developmental first, copyediting second, proofreading last. That sequence isn't editorial etiquette. It's about not paying for the same words twice.

A copyedit on a draft that's about to lose four chapters is money spent on sentences with no future. A proofread before a copyedit produces a manuscript that's correct and still clumsy, and every clumsy sentence the copyeditor then fixes needs proofreading again.

The one shortcut worth knowing: a copyedit ends with a proofreading pass, so those two are a single purchase here rather than two. The stage people skip and shouldn't is the honest read before the polish, and the stage people buy and often don't need is the second proofread on an already-clean file.

When you need less than you think

Some honest cases where the expensive answer is the wrong one.

  • A fourth draft that three sharp readers have already pulled apart. The developmental work happened. It just didn't have an invoice attached.
  • A 6,000-word business report. That's an afternoon's proofread, not a copyedit, and I'll price it as one.
  • A book that's already been copyedited well. If the file is clean, say so and I'll quote the proofread, not the upgrade.
  • A first novel you've been rewriting for four years. Occasionally the honest note is that more editing isn't what it needs, and somebody should tell you that before taking £500 off you.

The free sample edit exists partly for this. A thousand words usually makes the right level obvious to both of us, and it's much harder to sell somebody the wrong service when they can see the evidence.

Questions about the three edits

What is the difference between proofreading and copyediting?

Copyediting improves the writing. Proofreading corrects it.

A copyeditor rewrites clumsy sentences, cuts repetition, settles your spelling and hyphenation conventions, and flags the eye colour that changed in chapter nine. A proofreader takes a manuscript that has already been through that and catches what survived: typos, punctuation, a page number out of sequence, a running head still saying Chapter Four on page 210.

If nobody has copyedited the book yet, a proofread will make it correct without making it better.

Which one do I need for a self-published novel?

For most indie novels, a copyedit. It's the stage that includes a full proofread as its final pass, so it covers both jobs, and it's the one readers notice the absence of.

Add a developmental edit before it if you're unsure the story works, or if beta readers keep saying something vague about the middle. Add a separate proofread afterwards only if you're typesetting for print and want a fresh pass on the laid-out pages, where new errors get introduced by the layout itself.

Does a copyedit include a proofread?

Mine does. The final pass of every copyedit is a proofread, and you aren't billed for that stage twice.

Not every editor works this way, so it's worth asking before you compare two quotes. A cheaper copyedit that leaves you needing a separate proofread afterwards isn't cheaper.

What is line editing, and is it the same as copyediting?

Line editing is the sentence-level craft work: rhythm, imagery, voice, where a paragraph drags, which sentence should have been three. Copyediting is that plus the mechanical layer, meaning consistency, grammar, continuity, and a style sheet.

Some editors sell them separately. I don't, because splitting them means reading the same manuscript twice and charging you for the privilege. What I call copyediting includes the line work.

Can I skip developmental editing?

Very often, yes, and I'd rather say so than sell you one.

If you've drafted the book more than once, had it read by people who told you something you didn't want to hear, and acted on it, the structural work has probably already happened. Developmental editing earns its money on a first or second draft you're genuinely unsure about, or when several readers report the same vague dissatisfaction and none of them can name it.

I've run it through Grammarly and Word. Do I still need a proofreader?

Software catches misspellings and some grammar. It cannot tell you that your protagonist's sister is called Ellie in chapter three and Elsie in chapter nineteen, that you've used a single quotation mark in one line of dialogue on page 88, or that the word you wanted was "discreet".

It also can't tell the difference between a rule and your voice, which is why it keeps suggesting you flatten the sentences that make the book yours. Use it. Don't file after it.

My friend is an English teacher. Can't they proofread it?

They can, and they'll find real errors. Three things they usually won't do: keep a style sheet, so decisions drift across 300 pages; work in tracked changes, so you can't see or reject what changed; and tell you the book isn't ready, because they have to see you at Christmas.

The last one is the expensive one. Somebody with no stake in your feelings is worth more than somebody with excellent grammar.
