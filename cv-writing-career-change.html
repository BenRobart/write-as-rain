What applicant tracking software actually does

The popular version, where a robot scores your CV and bins it, is mostly wrong. Believing it leads people to do peculiar things, like hiding white keywords in the margins. Here's the accurate version, because it changes what you ought to do about it.

Most applicant tracking systems don't silently reject anybody on a score. They parse your CV into structured fields, meaning name, job titles, employers, dates, skills, education, then store the result so recruiters can search and filter the database later on.

So the real damage from bad formatting is parsing failure. If the system can't read your job titles and dates because they're sitting inside a text box or a two-column table, your record ends up garbled. A recruiter then searches for "project manager, Manchester, 5+ years" and you simply aren't in the results.

Nobody rejected you. You never surfaced. Which is worse, frankly, because there's no rejection email to tell you it happened.

What actually breaks parsing: multiple columns, frequently read straight across so two unrelated lines merge into nonsense. Tables. Text boxes. Headers and footers, often skipped entirely, which is where a great many people put their phone number. Icons used instead of words. Graphics and skill bars. Creative section headings like "Where I've Been" instead of "Experience". And PDFs that are secretly images of text.

Reframing, not reinventing

A career change CV has nothing to do with inventing experience you don't have. It's about describing the experience you do have in the vocabulary of the sector you're moving into, because the same work genuinely has two names and only one of them gets searched for.

A teacher moving into learning and development hasn't "taught Year 9 history". They've designed curricula, assessed learning outcomes, differentiated delivery for mixed-ability audiences, and managed stakeholder relationships with some of the most demanding stakeholders alive. A publican moving into operations has run stock, payroll, compliance, a team on rotating shifts, and a P&L.

None of that is a lie. It's identical work, described in the language the hiring manager searches with and thinks in.

Your raw material is the job advert itself. It tells you exactly which words the employer uses for the things they want, and a good CV echoes them. Not stuffed in artificially. Echoed because you genuinely did that thing and it's now labelled in a way they'd recognise.

The paragraph that explains the pivot

A career-change CV that doesn't address the change makes the reader do the work, and a puzzled reader moves on to the next file in the pile.

So the profile at the top has one job. It says what you're moving into, what you're bringing from where you've been, and why the move makes sense. Three or four lines.

"A passionate and dynamic professional seeking a new challenge" appears on roughly half the CVs in Britain and means nothing at all. What you want is one specific, plausible sentence about one specific move.

Achievements, with numbers

The commonest weakness in any CV, career change or otherwise, is listing what you were responsible for rather than what happened because you were there.

  • "Responsible for managing the team rota" → and what changed? Did absence drop? Did overtime spend fall? Did people stop leaving?
  • "Improved customer satisfaction" → from what to what, measured how, over how long?
  • "Managed a budget" → how big a budget? "Managed" and "£1.2m" are extremely different sentences.

Every "increased", "managed", "improved" and "supported" is begging the question by how much? If you honestly don't have the figure, an order of magnitude still beats nothing. "The largest of the four regional sites." "Roughly double the previous year."

Format, plainly

  • Two pages for almost everyone in the UK. One page if you're early-career. Academic CVs are the genuine exception and run long by design.
  • Single column, standard typeface, real headings. Boring parses cleanly and reads fast. Neither of your two readers cares about your design flair.
  • Reverse chronological with a targeted top. Profile and relevant skills, then a conventional history. A purely skills-based CV that hides dates reads as concealment to experienced recruiters.
  • Recent roles get the space. A job from 2009 needs one line, not the same six bullets as your current one.
  • No photo, no date of birth, no full postal address. UK convention, and the first two can complicate an employer's equal opportunities process rather than help you.

LinkedIn, saying the same thing

Recruiters look you up. Always. If your CV presents a considered move into operations while your LinkedIn headline still shows a job title from two roles ago, you've undone the work before anybody rings.

They aren't identical documents, mind. LinkedIn is first person, it can be warmer, and the About section has room for the story a CV can only gesture at. Rewritten together they tell one coherent version of you. That's the £120 option, and for anybody actively job-hunting it's the one worth having.

CV questions

Why am I not getting interviews?

For career changers, usually because the CV is about the job you had. A reader decides whether you're plausible for this role in a few seconds, and if the top of page one belongs to a different profession, that's settled before anybody reaches your transferable skills.

Second most common cause is duties where achievements should be. A list of responsibilities reads as somebody who occupied a role rather than somebody who did anything in it.

Do CVs really get rejected by software?

Not the way the myth has it, no. Most systems don't auto-reject on a score. They parse your CV into fields and store it so recruiters can search the database afterwards.

The real harm is parsing failure. If the software can't read your job titles and dates because they're sitting in a text box or a table, your record ends up garbled and you don't appear in the recruiter's search results at all.

Nobody rejected you. You never surfaced. Which is worse, because no email arrives to tell you it happened.

PDF or Word?

Do what the advert says. If it doesn't say, PDF is the safer default for modern systems and preserves your layout. If a portal looks like it was built in 2011, .docx is a reasonable hedge.

Far more important is what's inside the file. A PDF exported from a text document parses fine. A PDF that's really a scan or an image of a CV parses not at all – and looks, to the system, like an empty application.

How long should a CV be?

Two pages, in the UK, for almost everyone. One page if you're a graduate or very early career. Academic CVs are the real exception and run long by design.

If you're spilling onto three, the cause is nearly always that a job from fifteen years ago has the same six bullet points as your current one. It needs one line.

Should I use a template with columns and icons?

No. Those templates are built to look impressive in a preview, not to be read by software, and they cause the most common parsing failures.

  • Two columns often get read straight across, merging unrelated lines into gibberish.
  • Text boxes, headers and footers are frequently skipped – and that's where your phone number lives.
  • An icon instead of the word "Email" leaves an address with no label.
  • Skills rated four stars out of five tell a human nothing and a parser less.

Clean, single column, standard typeface. Dull, and it works.

Should I use a skills-based CV for a career change?

Not in its pure form. A fully functional CV that hides dates and employers reads to experienced recruiters as concealment – usually of a gap or a lack of relevant experience – and it makes verification harder.

A hybrid does the job: targeted profile and relevant skills at the top, conventional reverse-chronological history underneath. You get the reframing without the suspicion.

Do I still need a cover letter?

For a career change, yes, wherever one's accepted. It's the only place you can say directly why you're moving into this field and what you're bringing with you.

Without it, the recruiter has to infer your motivation from a CV that looks like a non-sequitur – and inference tends to go against the candidate. Under a page, and the first paragraph should explain the move, not announce which job you're applying for. They know which job.

Photo, date of birth, full address?

None of the three. UK convention leaves out photographs and dates of birth, and including them can complicate an employer's equal opportunities process rather than help you.

A full postal address isn't expected either – town or city, plus a note on commuting or relocating, is more useful. Marital status and your GCSE grades can go too, once you have a career behind you.
