Where you probably are right now

  • Eighteen submissions. Fourteen form rejections. Four silences that the agency website says also count as rejections.
  • Every one of them says "not quite right for my list", which tells you precisely nothing you can act on.
  • You've rewritten the query letter four times, following contradictory advice from four different corners of the internet.
  • You'd genuinely rather write another novel than write the one-page synopsis. You have considered this seriously.
  • There are maybe twenty agents left on your list and you're frightened of burning them.
  • You can no longer tell whether the book is any good. Not even roughly.

Diagnose it from the pattern, not the wording

Any individual rejection is meaningless. It's a template with your name pasted into it. The pattern across twenty of them, though, is genuinely diagnostic. Find yourself below.

Almost no requests for more material

If nearly everybody passes on the query alone, your problem is the query letter. Most agents never read past it, so the book isn't even in the room yet.

This is the most common situation by a distance, and it's also the cheapest to fix. Your novel is fine. You've been losing on the covering note.

Agents request pages, then pass

Pitch works, opening doesn't hold. Usually that means the first chapter starts in the wrong place. Too much scene-setting before anything happens, a protagonist who reacts instead of wanting something, or a voice that still hasn't asserted itself by page three.

It can also mean the writing is perfectly competent and unremarkable, which is a harder thing to hear and no harder to fix.

Agents request the full manuscript, then pass

You got through three gates. Query, opening and premise are all doing their jobs, so what's failing is the book, and nine times out of ten it's the middle. That long stretch where the opening question has been asked and the ending is still a very long way off.

That's a developmental problem. No amount of query polishing will lay a finger on it.

Nothing at all, ever, from anybody

Check the unglamorous possibilities first. Submissions that ignored the agency's stated format. Attachments where they asked for pasted text. A genre that agent doesn't represent. A word count so far outside the norm it read as a warning.

A surprising number of queries die on housekeeping, and nobody ever writes to tell you so.

The query letter, in the order agents read it

One page. Not one and a bit. Four parts.

  1. Housekeeping. Title, genre, word count, and one specific sentence on why this agent. Flattery isn't a reason. A book of theirs you've read is. So is a wishlist item you match, or a client whose work sits alongside yours.
  2. The pitch. Two paragraphs, maybe three. Who your protagonist is, what they want, what's in the way, what it costs them to fail. Write it in the tone of the novel, so the letter doubles as a sample of your voice.
  3. Comp titles. Two of them, published in roughly the last three to five years. Comps tell an agent where your book sits on a shelf and who's already buying it.
  4. You. Keep it short. Publication credits if you've got them, relevant expertise if the book leans on it. Nothing else. There's no requirement to be interesting.

Two things sink a query with dismal reliability. Describing the plot as a sequence of events rather than a situation with stakes. And claiming the book will appeal to "anyone who enjoys a good read", which manages to say nothing while also sounding slightly desperate.

A note on comp titles

Don't comp a mega-seller. "The next Harry Potter" reads as somebody who last looked at the market in 2012. Don't comp a book nobody has heard of either, because then the comparison conveys nothing whatsoever. And leave the classics alone.

What you want is a solid, recent, moderately successful book in your exact lane. Finding one is a research job, not a memory test, and it's worth an afternoon in a bookshop.

The synopsis, and why you hate it

You hate it because it asks you to be flat, complete and thoroughly unliterary about something you spent two years making subtle. Fair enough. But it isn't there to sell the book. It's there to prove the book holds together.

One page. Present tense. Character names in capitals on first appearance. The whole plot in order, subplots that matter included, subplots that don't left out. And the ending.

Withholding the ending to preserve suspense is the single most common synopsis error I see, and to an agent it reads as a writer who isn't entirely sure how their own book finishes.

A synopsis will rarely win you a request. It loses them regularly, because it's where an unresolved plot or a sagging middle becomes visible in about forty seconds.

Word count expectations, by genre

Being well outside your genre's range signals inexperience before anyone reads a sentence. These are rough debut guidelines, not laws:

CategoryUsual debut range
Adult literary & commercial fiction80,000 – 100,000
Crime & thriller80,000 – 100,000
Romance50,000 – 90,000
Science fiction & fantasy90,000 – 120,000
Young adult60,000 – 90,000
Middle grade30,000 – 55,000
Memoir & narrative non-fiction70,000 – 100,000

Under 60,000 words for adult fiction and over 130,000 for a debut are both real obstacles. Nobody is measuring your art. It's that both numbers correlate strongly with drafts that aren't finished yet, and agents have learned to read them that way.

UK and US conventions differ

UK agencies typically want a covering letter, a synopsis, and either the first three chapters or the first 10,000 words, usually submitted through a form on their own site. The tone runs a shade more restrained. The Writers' & Artists' Yearbook is still the standard reference for who represents what.

US agents want a query letter with the first five or ten pages pasted into the email body, and the culture around pitching is noticeably more forward. QueryTracker and agents' public wishlists do the job the Yearbook does over here.

Sending a US-style query to a London agency won't kill you. It's a small avoidable signal, and small avoidable signals add up when someone is reading their two hundredth submission of the week. Targeting both markets? The letter wants to exist in two versions.

Querying questions

Why do I keep getting rejected by agents?

Read the pattern, not the wording.

  • No requests at all. It's the query letter. Most agents never read past it.
  • Pages requested, then passed. It's your opening chapters.
  • Full manuscript requested, then passed. It's the book, and usually the middle of it.

Form rejections are deliberately uninformative. Twenty of them lined up together tell you far more than any single one ever will.

How long should a novel be to get an agent?

Roughly, for a debut: literary and commercial fiction 80–100k, crime and thriller 80–100k, romance 50–90k, SFF 90–120k, YA 60–90k, middle grade 30–55k, memoir 70–100k.

Under 60,000 for adult fiction reads as underwritten. Over 130,000 for a debut reads as unrevised. Neither is a rule, and both get read as signals before anybody opens the manuscript.

Do agents actually read the synopsis?

Some before the pages, some after, some only if the pages interested them.

It'll rarely win you a request. It loses them regularly, because a structural problem becomes visible in a synopsis in about forty seconds flat.

Include the ending. Always. Holding it back for suspense reads as a writer who doesn't know how their own book finishes.

How many agents should I query at once?

Batches of six to ten. Simultaneous submissions are standard and expected unless an agency specifically says otherwise.

Batching buys you information. If the query is the problem you find out after ten submissions rather than eighty, with most of your list still intact to approach with a better letter.

How long do agents take to reply?

Anywhere from a fortnight to six months. Many agencies now state plainly that silence after a given window means no.

Check each agency's submission page before you chase them, because chasing early is remembered. And keep a spreadsheet of who, when, what you sent and what came back. By submission thirty you won't remember, and querying the same agent twice by accident is a bad way to introduce yourself.

Should I self-publish instead?

They're two different routes. One isn't a consolation prize for failing at the other, whatever the internet occasionally implies.

Traditional publishing buys you bookshop distribution, prize eligibility, and an advance, at the cost of years of waiting and very little control. Self-publishing buys you speed, control, and far higher royalties per copy, at the cost of funding the editing, the cover, and the marketing yourself.

Genre fiction with a clearly defined readership does very well self-published. Romance above all. Literary fiction generally doesn't.

What actually goes in a query letter?

One page, four parts. Housekeeping (title, genre, word count, why this agent). The pitch (protagonist, want, obstacle, cost). Two recent comp titles. A short paragraph about you.

Write it in the tone of the novel so the letter doubles as a sample of your voice. That's the bit separating a competent query from one that gets read twice.

Do I need an agent to be published in the UK?

For the big houses, effectively yes. They don't take unagented submissions. Plenty of independent presses do accept direct approaches and some of them are excellent, typically paying smaller advances in exchange for a lot more editorial attention.

One rule hasn't changed in decades. A legitimate agent takes a commission from your earnings, after you've earned them. Anybody charging a reading fee, or nudging you towards a paid editorial service they happen to be connected to, isn't an agent worth having.
