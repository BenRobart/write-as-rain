What a language desk rejection actually means

It rarely means your English is bad. It means an editor did a quick calculation and concluded that reviewers would burn their limited goodwill on the prose instead of the science, then come back irritated.

And individual errors are seldom what triggers it. What triggers it is the argument being hard to follow. Sentences running to fifty words. Tense wandering between sections. Every claim hedged so thoroughly that the contribution vanishes. An abstract that never quite says what was found.

None of that requires changing a single number in a single table.

The boundary, stated plainly

Your data, your analysis, your interpretation, and your conclusions stay untouched. I don't strengthen a claim, weaken a caveat, or "improve" a finding.

Where a sentence can be read two ways, I don't pick one for you. There'll be a comment asking which you meant. Guessing at a scientific claim is precisely how meaning gets lost in editing, and it's the failure mode that makes researchers distrust editors in the first place.

Everything arrives as tracked changes or comments, so every alteration is visible and every alteration is reversible.

An editor is not an author. Improving the clarity of your English doesn't come close to meeting authorship criteria, and I'd never be listed as an author. Some journals do ask you to mention a language editor in the acknowledgements. Check your target journal's guidelines, and I'll help you word it if you'd rather not.

What I'm reading for

These are the patterns standing between a good paper and a readable one. None of them says anything about your English, incidentally. Several catch out native speakers just as often, and some catch them out more.

  • Sentence length. The single biggest cause of "unclear" comments. A finding split across two sentences beats the same finding crammed into one, nearly every time.
  • Tense discipline. Past tense for what you did and found, present for what's generally true, and one consistent convention in the discussion.
  • Articles. A, an, the, or nothing at all. English makes this distinction inconsistently and most other languages don't make it this way at all. It's the most common surface correction and easily the least interesting.
  • Hedging strength. "May possibly suggest that it could be the case that" is four hedges doing one hedge's job, and it makes a real finding sound like a guess.
  • Noun stacking. "Patient outcome improvement measurement protocol" is five nouns in a row that a reader has to unpack in their head. One preposition usually fixes it.
  • Signposting, so a reviewer skimming at eleven at night can still follow the argument.
  • The abstract. Read more than anything else you'll ever write, and frequently the weakest 250 words in the manuscript.

Formatting, references, and the things that bounce a paper

A manuscript can be rejected at the desk for reasons involving neither English nor science, which is a maddening way to lose a fortnight.

  • Reference style made consistent throughout, whether that's APA, Vancouver, Harvard, AMA, Chicago or the journal's own house variant.
  • In-text citations cross-checked against the reference list in both directions, so nothing is cited but missing, or listed but never cited.
  • Formatting brought into line with the author guidelines. Heading levels, word limits, abstract structure, keyword count, figure and table captions.
  • Figure and table numbering checked against the order they're first mentioned in the text.
  • Abbreviations defined at first use and then actually used, rather than alternating with the full term for forty pages.

The response to reviewers

Papers are won and lost here, and this letter gets a fraction of the attention the manuscript gets.

It has to do three things at once. Address every point raised. Show precisely where the manuscript changed. And stay courteous when a reviewer has plainly misread you.

That third one is the hard one. The most common mistake I see is answering a reviewer's misunderstanding as though it were an accusation, which is entirely understandable and demonstrably costs papers.

I'll edit the letter you've drafted, or help you build one point by point. Reviewer comment quoted, response given, change described, revised text quoted with a page reference. Editors read these fast and reward the ones that make their job easy.

Questions from researchers

Why was my paper rejected without peer review?

A desk rejection on language means an editor judged that reviewers would spend their effort on your writing rather than your research. It's an assessment of readability. It says nothing about your findings.

Individual errors are rarely the cause. Long sentences are. So is tense that shifts between sections, and hedging heavy enough that the contribution stops being visible. Fixing all of that requires changing exactly zero results.

How much does English editing for a paper cost?

From £15 per 1,000 words. A 6,000-word paper starts at £90, and a 9,000-word paper at £135.

Heavier work gets quoted once I've read the manuscript. The response-to-reviewers letter is quoted separately, because those range from one page to fifteen and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Either way, one fixed price before any paid work starts.

Will you change my results or my argument?

No. Data, analysis, interpretation and conclusions stay exactly as you wrote them.

Where a sentence could be read two ways, you'll get a comment asking which you meant rather than a decision made on your behalf. Everything arrives as a tracked change, so you can see and undo every alteration one by one.

Is it allowed to use a professional editor?

Yes. Language editing is standard practice and many publishers actively recommend it to authors writing in English as an additional language.

It's a long way from ghostwriting, and it isn't authorship either. An editor who improves clarity doesn't meet authorship criteria and shouldn't appear as an author. Some journals ask you to note it in the acknowledgements, so check your target journal's guidelines.

Can you provide a certificate of editing?

Yes, free, on request. A dated statement giving the manuscript title and the editing carried out, ready to upload with your resubmission.

Journals that reject on language grounds very often want exactly this before they'll look again, so mention it at the start if you think you'll need one.

Can you help with the response to reviewers?

Yes, and it's often where the most value sits. The letter has to answer every point raised, show exactly where the manuscript changed, and stay courteous when a reviewer has misread you.

That last part is the hard one. Answering a misunderstanding as though it were an accusation is the most common error I see, it's completely understandable, and it costs papers.

Do you need to be an expert in my field?

No, and expecting it slightly misunderstands the job. I'm not reviewing your methods or checking your claims. I'm making sure a reader can follow what you've written.

There's an argument that it helps to be outside the field. If I can't follow a passage, that's useful information, because a reviewer from a neighbouring subfield may not follow it either. Where a sentence genuinely depends on specialist knowledge, you'll get a query rather than a rewrite.

How long does it take?

A typical six-to-nine-thousand-word article takes a few working days, depending on what's already in the diary. You'll have a delivery date with the quote, before paying anything.

Up against a journal's resubmission deadline? Say so in your first email. You'll get a straight answer that day about whether it's achievable, rather than the two of us discovering it isn't in week two.
