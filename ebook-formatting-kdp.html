Send your file What usually breaks

How this stage usually goes wrong

  • The previewer puts a chapter heading halfway down a screen and you can't find what's causing it.
  • Your contents page works on a Kindle and does absolutely nothing in the phone app.
  • The paperback proof turns up with the inside text disappearing into the spine.
  • You uploaded the Word file. The italics survived; the scene breaks didn't.
  • The ebook is 38MB because one decorative flourish is sitting in the file thirty times over.
  • KDP rejected the print PDF for a trim size or bleed error, and the message explains nothing.
  • Every chapter starts on a right-hand page. Except three of them.

What's actually going wrong in the file

A Word document that looks immaculate on your screen is carrying a great deal you can't see, and conversion inherits every bit of it.

  • Direct formatting fighting styles. If a chapter heading is "18pt bold, centred" rather than Heading 1, no converter can tell it's a heading, and your navigation has nothing to build from.
  • Indents made of tabs and spaces. On a reflowing screen these land wherever they feel like.
  • Manual page breaks. Meaningful in print, meaningless in an ebook, and the cause of most half-empty screens.
  • Scene breaks made of blank lines. Whitespace collapses in conversion, so your quiet transition to a new scene simply disappears. Readers then wonder why the narrator changed continent mid-paragraph.
  • Tracked changes and comments still in the file. More common than you'd hope. Occasionally they end up published.
  • Curly and straight quotation marks mixed. Invisible in Word, obvious in a serif ebook font.

None of this is a criticism of your typing. Word is a word processor, and the file was never intended to become a book. It just needs rebuilding by somebody who knows which of its habits survive the trip.

The ebook file

EPUB 3, built to validate rather than merely to open. That distinction matters, because retailers accept files that technically pass and then render them oddly on half the devices in circulation.

  • One file for every store. Amazon retired MOBI for new uploads in 2021, so EPUB now covers KDP, Apple Books, Kobo, and Google Play alike.
  • Real navigation. A working table of contents plus the device-level chapter navigation, so the Go To menu on a Kindle behaves.
  • Semantic structure. Headings marked as headings, front matter marked as front matter. This is what makes the file behave predictably on hardware nobody has tested since 2016.
  • Scene breaks that survive. Marked as their own element rather than left as empty space.
  • Restrained typography. Drop caps and small caps that degrade gracefully instead of producing a giant letter floating on its own line.
  • Sensible file size. Images compressed properly, because on the 70% royalty option Amazon charges a delivery fee based on file size, and you pay it on every single sale.

Accessibility has stopped being optional. European rules that came in during 2025 mean ebooks sold into the EU are expected to meet accessibility requirements: proper heading structure, a declared language, alt text on meaningful images, and a logical reading order. The rules are still bedding in and the retailers keep updating their guidance, so I build to them by default rather than waiting to find out whose problem it is.

The print interior

Print is less forgiving than ebook, because there's no reader-side flexibility to save you. Every decision is final at the moment it's printed.

  • Trim size chosen before anything else. Everything downstream depends on it, and changing it later means resetting the whole interior.
  • Gutter scaled to page count. A 200-page book and a 500-page book need different inside margins, because a thicker book curves more at the spine. This is the single most common reason a proof arrives unreadable near the fold.
  • Mirrored margins so left and right pages are symmetrical rather than identical.
  • Fonts embedded in the PDF. Non-negotiable, and a frequent cause of rejection.
  • Page numbering that starts in the right place, with front matter handled separately and the first page of chapter one landing where a reader expects it.
  • Running heads that stay correct across the whole book, including the pages where convention says to suppress them.
  • Widows and orphans hunted down, and chapters opening on right-hand pages with the blank left-hand pages that implies.

KDP is fairly tolerant about the file it accepts. IngramSpark is fussier and wants a properly prepared PDF/X. Both change their specifications from time to time, so I check the current requirements against your chosen printer rather than working from memory.

Front and back matter, in the right order

Small thing, and one of the fastest ways a self-published book announces itself as self-published.

The front runs half title, title page, copyright page, dedication, then contents. The copyright page carries the year, the rights statement, your ISBN if you're using one, and the edition. It's dull and nobody reads it, right up until somebody does.

The back is where the money is. Acknowledgements, an also-by list that's actually complete, a short about-the-author, and a link to whatever you want the reader to do next while they still feel warmly about you. A reader who's just finished your book is the most valuable reader you will ever have, and a surprising number of books say nothing to them at all.

When you shouldn't pay me for this

If you publish several books a year, write straightforward narrative fiction with no images, and own a Mac, buy Vellum. It's a one-off cost, it produces genuinely handsome files, and by book four you'll be well ahead. Atticus does much the same thing in a browser if you're on Windows.

Paying per book makes sense when you publish occasionally, when the book has images, tables, footnotes, or anything else unusual, or when you would simply rather never learn what an EPUB is. That last one is a perfectly respectable reason and I'd never argue with it.

Formatting questions

Do I need to convert my book to MOBI for Kindle?

No. Amazon retired MOBI for new uploads back in 2021, and EPUB is now what you send them.

It's also what Apple Books, Kobo, and Google Play want, which means one properly built file covers every store rather than one file per shop. If a service is still offering to convert your book to MOBI, that tells you roughly how recently they updated their process.

Can't I just upload my Word document to KDP?

You can, and for a plain novel with no images it sometimes comes out acceptable.

What usually happens is that the conversion inherits everything invisible in the file: manual page breaks that land mid-screen on a phone, indents made with tabs or spaces, direct formatting fighting the styles, and scene breaks that vanish because they were three blank lines rather than a marked separator.

The result is a book that looks fine in the previewer on your laptop and wrong on somebody's Kindle. Nobody emails you about it. They just leave a review mentioning the formatting.

What's the difference between reflowable and fixed layout?

Reflowable means the text rewraps to the reader's screen, font size, and orientation, which is what you want for any book that's mostly words.

Fixed layout locks the page as a designed object, which is what children's picture books, cookbooks, poetry with fussy line breaks, and anything heavily illustrated need.

The prices on this page are for reflowable books. Fixed layout is genuinely a different job and gets quoted separately after I've seen what you've got.

Do I need an ISBN to self-publish?

Not for a Kindle ebook, which gets an ASIN from Amazon instead. For print, you need one, and you have two options.

KDP will give you a free one, with the catch that the imprint shows as Independently published and it can't be used with any other printer or distributor. Or you buy your own, in the UK through Nielsen, which lets you name your own imprint and keeps the ISBN yours wherever you print.

Each format needs its own: paperback, hardback, and ebook are three different ISBNs, not one.

Why did KDP reject my print PDF?

Nearly always one of four things:

  • The page size doesn't match the trim size you selected.
  • The margins or gutter are too small for the page count, which changes as the book gets thicker.
  • Fonts weren't embedded, so the printer can't reproduce them.
  • Bleed is set up wrong: either declared and unused, or used and undeclared.

Send me the file and the rejection message and I'll tell you which it is, free. Sometimes it's a five-minute fix you can do yourself, and I'd rather say so.

Can you format a book you didn't edit?

Yes, and plenty of the formatting work here is exactly that. Send the final file.

The one thing I'll ask is that it really is final, because formatting a manuscript that's still being edited means doing the job twice. If I spot obvious errors while working through it I'll flag them rather than silently correct them, since at that stage the words aren't mine to touch.

What happens when I want to change something later?

You get the source files, not just the finished ebook and PDF, so you're never locked in to me or to anyone.

Small corrections in the weeks after delivery, a typo caught by an early reader or a change to the also-by page, I just do. A new edition with substantial new content is a fresh job, and you'll get a number for it in advance like everything else.

Should I just use Vellum or Atticus instead?

Quite possibly, and I'd rather tell you that than take £75 from you.

If you're publishing several books a year, straightforward narrative fiction, and you own a Mac, Vellum pays for itself quickly and produces genuinely handsome files. Atticus does a similar job in a browser.

Paying somebody else per book makes sense when you're publishing occasionally, when the book has images, tables, footnotes, or anything unusual in it, or when you'd simply rather never think about EPUB again. That last reason is a perfectly good one.
