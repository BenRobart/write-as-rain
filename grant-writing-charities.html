The situation, as it usually is

  • The application is being written at half past ten at night by somebody who delivered the service all day.
  • You were rejected with no feedback whatsoever, and the fund won't discuss it.
  • You know the work is good. You cannot get that onto the form.
  • The guidance runs to fourteen pages. The questions have 250-word limits.
  • They've asked for "outcomes" and you're now fairly sure you've been giving them outputs for years.
  • Someone offered to write bids for a percentage of whatever you win. It felt wrong and you couldn't say why.

The guidance is a scoring rubric. Read it as one.

Here's the single most useful shift in perspective available to a small charity. An assessor isn't reading your application as a story about your organisation. They're working down a list of criteria assigning marks, usually against a rubric they didn't write and can't deviate from.

So a beautifully written paragraph that doesn't address a criterion scores zero. Not "a bit less". Zero. Meanwhile a plain, slightly clunky paragraph that answers the criterion directly and brings evidence scores full marks.

Which is why the first thing I do with any fund is turn the guidance into a checklist of everything being scored, then make certain every item on it is answered somewhere the assessor will actually find it. Not implied. Not covered elsewhere in the form. Answered, in the box where they're looking for it.

The most expensive habit in the sector is answering the question you wish they'd asked. You have a genuinely moving story about your founder, and the question is about governance arrangements. The story scores nothing, and it eats the words you needed for the actual answer.

Outputs and outcomes, since this loses more money than anything else

Outputs are what you deliver. Outcomes are what changes because you delivered it.

  • Output: "40 sessions for 120 young people." Outcome: "Participants report increased confidence, and school attendance across the group rises."
  • Output: "A weekly lunch club for 30 older residents." Outcome: "Reduced isolation, measured on a wellbeing scale at intake and again at six months."
  • Output: "500 food parcels distributed." Outcome: "Households stabilised long enough to engage with debt advice."

Funders are buying outcomes and they score them heavily. Yet most weak applications describe outputs in loving detail and leave the outcomes vague and aspirational, which is understandable. Outputs are what you can count on a Friday afternoon. Outcomes are what you hope is happening.

The strong version names the outcome, says how it's measured, and is honest about the timescale. "We expect to see X within 12 months, measured by Y" beats "the project will transform lives" every time, for the unromantic reason that an assessor can put a number next to the first one.

The case for support, in three parts

  1. The need, evidenced rather than asserted, and local. National statistics establish that a problem exists. They don't establish that it exists on your street. Ward-level data, your own waiting list, a survey you ran, a partner agency's referral numbers. That's what makes need real to somebody who has already read forty applications quoting the same national picture.
  2. The response. What you'll actually do, concrete enough that a reader can picture a Tuesday. Who does it, where, how often, for whom, and what it costs.
  3. Why you. The bit small organisations undersell most badly. You have things a large charity hasn't got: trust that took a decade to build, reach into communities that don't answer official letters, and a cost base a fraction of the size. Those are competitive advantages. Put them in the form.

Budgets that match the words

An assessor who reads a narrative about three members of staff and then finds two in the budget has just been handed a reason to mark you down. They will use it.

So the budget gets checked line by line against the narrative. Every activity described has a cost attached, every cost has an activity, and the arithmetic works. Beyond that:

  • Claim your core costs wherever the funder permits it. Small organisations chronically under-claim overheads, out of a vague sense that asking for rent and management time is somehow greedy. It isn't. It's how you stay solvent, and funders who allow full cost recovery fully expect to see it claimed.
  • Justify anything unusual in one sentence, rather than leaving an assessor guessing why a line item looks the way it does.
  • Be realistic. An underpriced project reads as inexperience, and it's the project you'll then have to go and deliver.

How I charge, and why it matters

Fixed fee for the work, agreed in advance, paid whatever the outcome. Never a percentage of what you win.

Two reasons for that. UK fundraising guidance advises against commission based on funds raised, and many funders won't allow their grant money to pay a percentage-based fee in the first place.

The second reason is plainer. A writer on commission has every incentive to nudge you towards every fund in sight, and no incentive at all to tell you one of them is a poor fit. That points the wrong way, and small organisations are precisely the people who can least afford it to.

Grant writing questions

Why do our applications keep getting rejected?

Most often because the application describes the organisation instead of answering the questions. Assessors score against published criteria, and a moving answer to a question nobody asked scores nothing at all.

After that, roughly in order: outputs supplied where outcomes were wanted, a fund whose priorities never matched the project, a budget that doesn't match the narrative, and a question left half-answered when the word limit ran out.

Only the last of those is really about writing. The rest come down to reading the guidance as a scoring rubric, which almost nobody does.

Do you work on commission or take a percentage?

No. And be wary of anybody who offers to.

UK fundraising guidance advises against commission based on funds raised, and many funders won't permit their grant to pay a percentage-based fee anyway.

There's a plainer objection too. A writer on commission has every incentive to push you towards every fund going, and none whatsoever to tell you one isn't worth applying for. Fixed fee for the work, whatever the outcome, keeps everybody honest.

What's the difference between outputs and outcomes?

Outputs are what you deliver. Outcomes are what changes as a result.

"40 sessions for 120 young people" is an output. "Those young people report improved confidence, and school attendance rises" is an outcome.

Funders buy outcomes and score them heavily. The strongest applications name the outcome, say how it's measured, and are honest about the timescale – because all three can be scored, and "will transform lives" can't.

Can you help with a National Lottery Awards for All application?

Yes. Awards for All covers smaller community grants – up to £20,000 in England – and the form is short enough that people assume it's easy.

The brevity is the difficulty. You have to make a complete case for need, activity, outcome, and budget inside tight limits. It consistently rewards three things: evidence the community actually asked for this, a clear description of who benefits, and a budget that visibly matches the narrative.

Will you write it, or help us write it?

Either. Some organisations want a complete draft built from a conversation and their existing documents. Others have a draft and want it restructured against the criteria, cut to the limits, and strengthened where the evidence is thin.

The second is cheaper and often more useful – the knowledge of your work sits with you and can't really be outsourced. What I can't do either way is invent your evidence, so I'll need your real numbers, real beneficiary feedback, and real accounts.

How long does a funding application take?

One to two weeks for a short community grant, including your review time. Three to four for a larger trust application or a tender with method statements.

The limiting factor is almost never the writing – it's waiting on monitoring data, accounts, policies, and quotes from your side. Give me the deadline first and I'll tell you honestly whether it's achievable.
