Signs it's the blurb and not the book

  • The cover's good, the reviews are good, and people still aren't clicking Buy.
  • Yours reads as a plot summary in chronological order, complete with a thing nobody should know until page 200.
  • It runs to 400 words. Readers left somewhere around 60.
  • Every noun has two adjectives in front of it and nothing has happened yet.
  • You've rewritten it fourteen times and can no longer read it as words at all.
  • The first line is a rhetorical question you would never ask a stranger holding your book in a shop.
  • You know exactly what your novel is about and can't say it in a sentence, which is entirely normal after two years inside it.

A blurb is not a summary of your book

This is the mistake underneath most weak blurbs, and it's a completely understandable one. You've been asked what happens in your novel so many times that summarising it has become a reflex.

But a summary answers questions. A blurb creates them. The reader isn't trying to find out what happens, they're trying to decide whether they want to. Those are opposite jobs, and one of them requires you to leave things out on purpose.

The other reflex worth breaking: writing for people who already care. Your beta readers, your writing group, and your mum all approach the blurb knowing the characters. A stranger arrives knowing nothing and owing you nothing, and every name you introduce is a small tax on their patience.

The shape that works

Almost every blurb that does its job has the same four moves in it, whatever the genre.

  1. A hook line. One sentence, standing alone, that contains the tension of the whole book. If a friend asked what you're reading, this is the sentence that would make them say "go on".
  2. Who, and what their normal is. Two sentences at most. One protagonist, named. Everybody else is "her brother" or "the new detective inspector", because a reader will hold two names and resent a fifth.
  3. What breaks it. The disruption, and crucially what it costs. Not the plot's whole machinery, just the thing that makes staying still impossible.
  4. The question, left open. A final line that points at the stakes without resolving them. This is where a lot of blurbs panic and start explaining.

Length: 100 to 150 words for a back cover, where you're sharing physical space with a review quote and a barcode. Around 150 to 250 for Amazon. The field permits 4,000 characters, and using them is a mistake nobody has been punished for enough.

The first two lines are the whole game on a phone. That's all anyone sees before the Read more link on a mobile product page, and most of your browsers are on a phone. Any blurb that spends its opening on scene-setting has spent the only part guaranteed to be read.

What Amazon does with the description

The description field on KDP isn't the same object as your back cover, and treating them as one file is how people end up with a print blurb that reads oddly online, or an online one that won't fit the cover.

  • Formatting is allowed, within limits. Bold, italics, headings, line breaks, and simple lists are supported, and KDP's own toolbar will write the markup for you. Anything outside the permitted set gets stripped, or displayed as visible tags, which looks exactly as bad as it sounds.
  • White space is doing work. A wall of text on a product page reads as effort. Short paragraphs, one idea each, and the hook on its own line.
  • The bits underneath. A series line ("Book two of the Havering Mysteries, and it stands alone"), a comparison line for readers who need a reference point, and an author bio that's three sentences rather than a CV.
  • A+ Content, where you're eligible for it. Extra image and text modules further down the page. Worth having, and no substitute for the description itself, which is what people read first.

Once the words are right, the file has its own set of requirements, and those are a separate job with separate ways to go wrong.

Lines that cost you readers

  • "In a world where..." Instantly places your book among a thousand others that opened the same way. Say the specific thing about your world instead.
  • Five names in 120 words. The reader isn't taking notes. One name, maybe two.
  • "Little did she know..." Withholding is fine. Announcing that you're withholding is not.
  • Three rhetorical questions in a row. One can work in some genres. Three reads as a blurb that couldn't find a statement to make.
  • "A gripping, unputdownable, heart-stopping thriller." That's the reader's verdict to reach, and claiming it in advance invites them to disagree. Show the thing that grips instead.
  • The exciting bit from chapter nineteen. Selling one page and spoiling three hundred.
  • "This book will take you on a journey." Reviews are allowed to say that. Blurbs aren't.

Signalling the genre promise

Readers buy a promise before they buy a story, and each genre's promise has its own vocabulary. Miss it and you get the worst possible outcome: the wrong readers buying your book and then reviewing it for not being what they expected.

  • Romance. The tropes belong in the blurb, plainly. Enemies to lovers, forced proximity, second chance. Heat level signalled by register rather than announced. The happy ending is promised, never described.
  • Crime and thriller. Who's under threat, what the clock is, and the specific texture of the investigation. "A body is found" is not a hook. Whose body, found by whom, and what that costs them is.
  • Fantasy and sci-fi. Enough of the world to make it feel particular, without a paragraph of proper nouns. One invented term, at most, and only if it's doing real work.
  • Memoir and non-fiction. The promise is what the reader comes away with. For memoir especially, the blurb has to sell a person, not a chronology.

Blurb questions

How long should a book blurb be?

For a back cover, 100 to 150 words. Print gives you a physical space to fit, usually shared with a review quote and a barcode.

For Amazon, 150 to 250 words does the work, even though the field allows up to 4,000 characters. Longer descriptions aren't rewarded; they're skimmed.

What matters far more than the total is the first two lines, because on a phone that's all anyone sees before the Read more link.

What's the difference between a blurb, a synopsis, and a description?

A blurb sells. A synopsis explains.

The blurb is the marketing copy on the back cover and on your retailer page, and it withholds on purpose. A synopsis is the document an agent asks for, it covers the whole plot including the ending, and no reader should ever see it. Description is simply Amazon's name for the blurb field.

Authors get into trouble by pasting something synopsis-shaped into that field and wondering why nobody clicks.

Should the blurb give away the ending?

No. A blurb's job is to raise the question, not answer it.

It should be honest about what kind of book this is, which is a different thing entirely: a romance blurb promises the happy ending, a crime blurb promises resolution, and neither names who did it.

The most common self-inflicted wound is describing an event from chapter nineteen because it's the most exciting thing in the book. That sells one page and spoils three hundred.

Can I use HTML in my Amazon book description?

A limited set of tags, yes. Bold, italics, headings, line breaks, and simple lists are supported, and KDP also gives you a formatting toolbar that writes the markup for you. Everything else gets stripped or, worse, displayed as raw tags on your product page.

Sensible formatting genuinely helps, because it gives the eye somewhere to land. Six different heading sizes and a wall of bold does the opposite.

Check the current KDP guidance before you paste anything in, since the accepted tags change occasionally.

Do you need to read the whole book?

Not always, but I need more than a summary. Usually the first three chapters, the last one, and a paragraph from you on what the book is really about, which is often not what happens in it.

If I've copyedited the manuscript I already have all of that, which is why the blurb costs less when it's added to an edit.

What I can't work from is a one-line pitch, because the specific detail that makes a blurb land is always somewhere in the actual text.

Will you write a blurb for a book you haven't edited?

Yes, and it's a common request. Plenty of authors are perfectly happy with their editor and simply can't write about their own book, which is a completely normal condition after eighteen months inside it.

You send the manuscript, the cover if it exists, and two or three comparable titles you'd want to sit beside. Nothing about the book itself gets criticised unless you ask, though if I spot something that will hurt sales I'll mention it once.

Will a better blurb actually sell more copies?

Nobody honest will promise you that, and anyone quoting a percentage uplift is quoting somebody else's book.

What can be said plainly: the cover earns the click, the blurb earns the sample, and the sample earns the sale. A weak blurb loses readers who were already interested enough to look, which is the most expensive kind of reader to lose.

Fixing it removes an obstacle. It doesn't manufacture demand that was never there.
