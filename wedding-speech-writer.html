Occasions · Wedding speeches

Start with a chat How a speech is built

Best man, father of the bride, maid of honour: the dread is identical

  • You said yes eleven months ago, when it was an abstract concept happening to a future version of you.
  • You've opened the document six times. You've written "So, Dave" six times.
  • Everything genuinely funny about him is also genuinely unrepeatable in front of his mother.
  • You're the father of the bride, and every draft so far reads like a reference for your daughter rather than a speech about her.
  • You're not sure whether you're supposed to thank people. Or which people.
  • You're not a public speaker. You don't want to become one. There are ninety chairs.

It starts with a conversation, not a form

Half an hour on the phone. Forty-five minutes if you're enjoying yourself. I ask about him. How you met, what he was like then, what changed when he met her, the thing he does that everybody who knows him recognises instantly.

Most of what makes a speech land arrives sideways during that conversation, usually in the bit after you've finished the anecdote you meant to tell. It's almost never the story you'd have written down if I'd emailed you a questionnaire. Which is why I don't email anybody a questionnaire.

I'm also listening to how you talk. Your rhythm, your vocabulary, whether you're dry or warm, the phrases you use constantly without noticing. A speech that sounds like a professional speechwriter has failed, because everybody in that room knows you and they'll hear the seam within a sentence.

The shape that works

There's a structure under most good wedding speeches, and it exists because of what it does to a room.

  1. Open with something that isn't "for those of you who don't know me". One line that grabs the room and buys you the next thirty seconds.
  2. Establish who you are to him. Briefly. They need to know why you're the one standing up.
  3. The stories. Two, maybe three. Specific, properly told, with an ending. One long well-told story beats four half-remembered ones every single time.
  4. The turn. Where it stops being funny and becomes true, usually about what changed when he met her. This is the part people remember afterwards, and it's the part most speeches skip entirely.
  5. The toast. Short and clear, so everybody knows to raise a glass without that awkward pause where nobody's sure if you've finished.

What that shape does is take a room that's been drinking since one o'clock, get it laughing, then land something sincere while its guard is down. Comedy first, sincerity second. It does not work the other way round.

Five minutes. Seven at the absolute outside. That's roughly 600 to 800 words, because people speak more slowly than they read, and considerably more slowly when nervous. Nobody in recorded history has left a wedding complaining that a speech was too short.

The line between funny and cruel

One test, and it's reliable. Would he happily retell this story himself, to this room, with these people in it?

If the answer needs a qualifier, if it's "well, probably", it doesn't go in. That room contains grandparents, colleagues, small children, and in-laws who have never met you and are forming their entire impression of you over the next five minutes.

Permanently out: exes. Anything about the couple's sex life. Genuinely private things he told you in confidence. And any joke whose humour rests on him being humiliated rather than fondly recognised.

The gap between affectionate mockery and public embarrassment is obvious in a room and nearly invisible on a page. Which is exactly why it's worth having somebody else read the thing first.

Reading the room you'll actually be in

Tell me who's going to be there and the speech changes. A wedding heavy on his colleagues needs different material from one that's almost all family. Children present constrains things. A recent bereavement usually has one sentence that acknowledges it warmly, and a great many sentences that get it wrong.

Blended families. Stepparents. Complicated relatives. Somebody who very much should be mentioned and somebody who very much shouldn't. All of that is normal, all of it is solvable, and it's far easier solved at the writing stage than at the lectern.

Delivery notes, because the writing is only half of it

A good speech badly delivered is a bad speech. So yours arrives marked up.

  • Where to pause. Particularly the pause before a punchline, which is where the laugh actually comes from.
  • Where to look up, and at whom.
  • Which lines to slow right down for, and which to get through briskly.
  • Where a laugh should be, so you know to wait for it instead of talking over the top of it.
  • What to do with your hands, and where to put the cards down.

Print it larger than you think you need to. Number the cards. Read it aloud at least three times, ideally to somebody else, because the first read always turns up a sentence that works on paper and dies in your mouth. Tell me which one and I'll change it.

Not just the best man

  • Father of the bride. More weight, less obligation to be funny, and the real difficulty is restraint. Which memory to tell, and which three to leave out. Usually carries hosting duties as well.
  • The groom, or both partners. Thanking people without it turning into a register, and saying something about your new spouse that isn't a greetings card.
  • Maid of honour and bridesmaids. Increasingly common, and freer, because the form hasn't calcified yet.
  • Mothers, stepparents, siblings, family friends. For weddings that don't match the traditional template, which is most of them now.
  • Second marriages, where the tone is genuinely different and often warmer, and where a template will let you down badly.

Wedding speech questions

How long should a best man speech be?

Five minutes. Seven is the ceiling. That's roughly 600–800 words, because you'll speak more slowly than you read – nerves do that.

The classic error is writing to length on the page instead of timing it out loud, which is how a speech that looked like six minutes becomes eleven on the night. Time it with a stopwatch, standing up.

How much does a wedding speech writer cost?

From £100 – including the conversation, the written speech timed to your slot, delivery notes, and a revision after you've read it aloud.

If you've already drafted something and just want it made to work, that's less. Complicated situations get quoted after we've talked.

What jokes should I avoid?

Exes. The couple's sex life. Anything genuinely private he told you in confidence. And any joke that only works if he's humiliated rather than fondly recognised.

The test: would he happily tell this story himself, to this room, with these people in it? If that needs a "probably", it's out. There are grandparents in there, and in-laws who are meeting you for the first time.

How far in advance should I book?

Four to six weeks is comfortable – enough for the conversation, the draft, a revision, and rehearsal that isn't panicked.

Shorter notice usually works, and last-minute requests are far more common than you'd think, so ask anyway. Booking much earlier than two months doesn't help much: the best anecdotes tend to arrive during the stag do.

Will it sound like me?

That's the whole point of doing it by conversation rather than questionnaire. I'm listening to your rhythm and vocabulary as much as to your stories.

A speech that sounds like a speechwriter has failed – everyone in that room knows you and will hear it. If a line doesn't sound like you when you read the draft aloud, say so and it goes.

Can you write a father of the bride speech?

Yes – and it's a genuinely different job. More emotional weight, less obligation to be funny, and the difficulty is restraint: which memory to tell, and which three to leave out.

It usually carries hosting duties as well, welcoming guests and thanking people. Same goes for mothers, stepparents, and anyone else taking that role in a family that doesn't match the traditional template.

I'm not funny. Can you still help?

Yes, and it's worth saying plainly: a wedding speech doesn't have to be funny to be good.

The ones people remember are the ones that were true about the person. A sincere, well-built five minutes from someone who clearly loves the couple beats a run of jokes from someone visibly hoping to survive. If comedy isn't your register, we won't force it, and the speech will be better for that decision.

What's the running order for wedding speeches?

Traditionally: father of the bride, then the groom, then the best man, after the meal.

Plenty of weddings depart from that now – brides speak, both partners speak, maids of honour speak, and some couples move speeches before the meal so the speakers can eat without dread. Any order works if everyone knows it beforehand.

What matters more is the total. Three speeches at eight minutes each is a long stretch for a room that's been sitting since two.
