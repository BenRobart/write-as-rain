Occasions · Eulogies

Help writing a eulogy

You said yes because you couldn't say no. The funeral is soon. You've opened the document several times and you can't get past the first line.

One phone call is enough. You talk about them; I'll do the writing. You don't need to have your thoughts in order first – that's the part I can take off you.

Get in touch

Short notice is normal

Most funerals are arranged inside a week or two, and most people are asked to speak with very little warning. So a service in three days isn't an imposition. It's the usual situation.

If that's where you are, say so in your first message. You'll get a straight answer the same day about whether it can be done. Usually it can.

How it works

We talk for about half an hour, whenever suits you. You don't need to prepare anything or bring notes.

I ask questions, you answer them, and the answers don't have to be organised or even particularly coherent. Most of what ends up in a eulogy arrives sideways. In a detail mentioned in passing. In something you start saying and then stop. That's normal, and it's why a conversation works better than a form.

Then I write it and send it over. If a line isn't right, or a story sits wrong, say so and I'll change it. There's no limit on that and no charge for it.

If you'd rather write it yourself and just need help getting started, that's fine. If you've written something and got stuck, also fine. Send whatever you have, in whatever state it's in. It doesn't need tidying up first.

What a eulogy actually needs

Not a list of dates. A biography of where somebody was born, where they worked, and who they married is entirely accurate and sounds nothing like a person.

What works is smaller. Two or three things:

  • A habit anyone who knew them would recognise the moment they heard it.
  • A moment that shows who they were, rather than a sentence claiming it.
  • Something they always said, said the way they said it.

Specific details are what make a room recognise someone. One sentence about how they answered the phone will do more than a paragraph about their character, and it's usually the thing that makes people laugh and cry at the same time. Which is rather what a eulogy is for.

It doesn't have to cover everything. It was never going to be a complete account of a life. Leaving things out isn't a failure.

Three to five minutes

Roughly 400 to 700 words. Shorter than most people expect, and right for two reasons.

One is practical. Funerals run to a slot, usually with other contributions to fit around yours.

The other matters more. A short eulogy is much easier to get through when you're struggling, because there's less distance between the beginning and the end.

Getting through it on the day

Almost everyone is frightened of breaking down. It's worth planning for rather than hoping against.

Your eulogy will be written in short sentences with natural stopping points, and pause marks placed where you're most likely to need one. Alongside that, a few things that genuinely help:

  • Print it large. Larger than feels sensible. Grief does odd things to reading.
  • Have a glass of water, and use it. Stopping for a drink is an ordinary thing to do.
  • At each pause mark, take a full breath before carrying on. Don't push through.
  • Give a copy to somebody else beforehand and agree that if you can't go on, they'll step up and finish it. No signal needed. They'll know.

That last one matters more than the rest of them together. Knowing the arrangement exists is very often what makes it unnecessary.

And if you don't get through it, nobody in that room will think less of you. Not one person.

When the relationship was complicated

Not everybody is easy to write about, and pretending otherwise produces a eulogy that sounds like a stranger. The room usually knows.

You can speak well of someone without claiming they were straightforward. There's nearly always a way to say something true, warmly and without detail, that the people who understand will hear properly and everybody else will simply hear as kindness.

Worth talking through rather than wrestling with alone at one in the morning. It's one of the more common reasons people get in touch, and there's nothing unusual about it at all.

Questions people ask

How long should a eulogy be?

Three to five minutes – roughly 400 to 700 words. Shorter than most people expect.

Partly because funerals run to a slot. Mostly because a short eulogy is far easier to get through when you're struggling: there's less distance between the start and the end. Nobody has ever felt a eulogy was too brief.

How quickly can you write one?

Usually within a day or two of speaking, and faster if the funeral is close.

Short notice is the normal situation here, not an imposition. If the service is in three days, say so and you'll get a straight answer immediately.

What should a eulogy include?

Who they were, rather than what they did. Dates and job titles are accurate and sound nothing like a person.

Two or three specific things work better: a habit everyone would recognise, a moment that shows their character, something they always said. It can acknowledge the people they've left behind.

It doesn't have to include everything. It isn't a complete account of a life, and leaving things out isn't a failure.

What if I can't get through it on the day?

It's common, and it's planned for. Short sentences, natural stopping points, and pause marks where you're most likely to need one.

Print it large. Have water. Breathe fully at each pause instead of pushing on. And give a copy to someone else beforehand, with an agreement that they'll step up and finish it if you can't – no signal needed.

Knowing that arrangement exists is very often what makes it unnecessary. And if it does happen, nobody there will think less of you.

The relationship was complicated. Can you still help?

Yes. And a eulogy that sounds nothing like the person everyone actually knew is worse than one that's quietly truthful.
