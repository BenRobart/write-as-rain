Non-fiction · Memoir & life story

A life is not a plot, and everything that happened is too much material

Which is the real problem with memoir, and it isn't a writing problem. You have eighty years and no idea which fifteen of them the book is about. Fixing that is most of the work, and it's the part that turns a long account of events into something a stranger will read to the end.

Where memoirs get stuck

  • Ninety thousand words that stop rather than end, because life didn't provide a final chapter.
  • You began at birth, since that's where it began, and the part people lean forward for is on page 140.
  • Everyone says "you should write a book". That's affection, not feedback, and it tells you nothing about the draft.
  • Half the people in it are still alive, and you haven't decided how honest you're allowed to be.
  • You stopped writing four months ago over whether it was 1978 or 1979.
  • There's a box: letters, photographs, your mother's diaries. No shape has emerged from it yet.
  • Your children will read this, and that thought is sitting on top of every sentence you write.

What the book is about, as opposed to what happened

An autobiography is organised by chronology. A memoir is organised by meaning, and that single difference decides whether a reader stays.

So the first question in any developmental read here is blunt: what is this book asking? Not what happened, which you know perfectly well, but the question the reader is being carried through. How a marriage survived something. What it cost to leave. Why you spent thirty years doing a job you never chose.

The same life yields four completely different books depending on that answer, and the material that belongs in one of them is dead weight in the others. Once the question is settled, most of the structural decisions make themselves, including the cheerful business of cutting 20,000 words that were only there because they were true.

Where to start, and where to stop

Chronology is a default, not a structure. Starting at birth is the commonest opening in unpublished memoir, and it forces the reader through a decade of context before anything is at stake.

The stronger opening is the moment the question becomes live: the letter arriving, the diagnosis, the day you decided. Childhood then arrives later, as explanation for something the reader already wants explained, which is a much easier sell than childhood arriving first as homework.

Endings are the other half of it. A life continues, so a memoir has to end on the question rather than on the calendar. The last chapter of a draft is very often chapter twenty-two rather than chapter twenty-nine, and it's usually visible from the outside long before it's visible from the inside.

Scene, summary, reflection

Three gears, and most drafts are stuck in one of them.

  • Summary covers ground. "We moved to Coventry in 1971 and things were difficult for a while." Necessary, and if the book is all summary it reads as a long report.
  • Scene puts the reader in the room. One afternoon, one conversation, in the present tense of the telling. This is where readers form attachments, and it's what most first drafts are short of.
  • Reflection is the gear only memoir has: you now, looking back at you then, and knowing something that person didn't. Without it, a memoir is just an anecdote with dates.

That double vision is what separates memoir from a chronicle, and getting the proportions right is most of what a developmental edit on a memoir actually does. In the margin you'll see a great many notes reading "this is summary, and it deserves to be a scene", along with rather more useful ones telling you which three scenes are doing the same job so two can go.

Memory, and being honest about it

People stall for months over whether something happened in 1978 or 1979. It rarely matters, and there's an established way to handle it.

Readers don't require perfect recall. What they won't forgive is false precision: the flawlessly remembered forty-line conversation from half a century ago. Uncertainty on the page is a strength, oddly enough. "I remember it as a Tuesday, though my sister is certain it was the weekend" builds more trust than either version asserted flatly, and it's a sentence only a real person could write.

Practical measures, where it matters: check dates against the records you do have, letters, certificates, employment history. Say plainly in an author's note that dialogue is reconstructed. And where the family disagrees about an event, putting the disagreement in the book is nearly always more interesting than picking a side.

The people who are still alive

This is the part people arrive most worried about, and the worry is reasonable. It's also manageable, and knowing the shape of the risk removes most of the fear.

  • You cannot defame the dead in England and Wales. Writing honestly about a late parent carries no defamation risk from them. A living relative who's identifiable in the same passage is a separate question.
  • Truth is a defence, but only where you could actually demonstrate it. "It's true" and "I can show it's true" are different positions.
  • Privacy is not the same as defamation. A true fact about someone's health, finances, or sex life can still cause you problems. This one surprises almost everybody.
  • Changing names does very little if the person remains identifiable from everything around them. Composite characters help more, and they need declaring.
  • Quoted material needs permission. Song lyrics above all, where quoting one line is quoting a large share of the work and rights holders price it accordingly. Titles are free to use, so name the song and describe what it did to the room.

I'm an editor, not a solicitor, and this page isn't legal advice. What I do is mark every passage where a publisher's legal read would stop, explain what's triggering it, and suggest ways to keep what's true while lowering your exposure. Where something genuinely needs a lawyer, you'll be told that clearly rather than reassured.

The family memoir nobody's selling

Plenty of the life stories I work on will never have an ISBN. They're twenty or thirty thousand words, printed a dozen times, and given to grandchildren who'll read them properly in about fifteen years.

These are worth doing well and they're a different job from a trade memoir. Shorter is better, since forty pages get read twice and four hundred get shelved. Photographs and documents earn their place. Names, dates, and places matter more than they would in a book aimed at strangers, because the readers are checking.

If writing it yourself isn't realistic, the usual route is conversation. We talk, recorded, in sessions of about an hour. That gets transcribed and shaped into chapters, and you read and correct every draft, because the point of the exercise is that it sounds like the person it's about.

One practical note, said plainly because it comes up. If the person whose story this is happens to be ill or very old, say so in your first message. We start with the recording and worry about the shape of the book afterwards. The recording is the part that can't be done later.

Memoir questions

What's the difference between a memoir and an autobiography?

An autobiography covers a life, start to date, and generally assumes the reader already cares who you are. A memoir takes a slice of a life and uses it to say something. One is organised by chronology, the other by meaning.

Almost everyone who tells me they're writing an autobiography is actually writing a memoir, and the moment they stop trying to include everything, the book gets better and considerably shorter.

How long should a memoir be?

For a memoir aimed at publication, 70,000 to 90,000 words is the comfortable range, and a debut running past 120,000 will meet resistance from agents.

For a private family book, 20,000 to 40,000 is plenty, and shorter is very often better: the grandchildren are more likely to read forty pages twice than four hundred pages once.

Length is the wrong thing to worry about first. What the book is about is the right thing.

Can I write about my family without being sued?

Usually, and carefully. I'm an editor rather than a solicitor, so this isn't legal advice, but the shape of it is worth knowing.

In England and Wales you cannot defame the dead, so writing honestly about a late parent carries no defamation risk from them, though a living relative who is identifiable in the same passage is a different matter. Truth is a defence, but only if you could actually demonstrate it. Privacy is a separate issue from defamation, which surprises people: a true fact about someone's health, sex life, or finances can still cause you trouble. And changing a name does very little if the person is identifiable from the details around it.

What I do is mark the passages where a publisher's legal read would stop, explain what's causing the flag, and suggest ways to keep the truth while lowering the exposure. Where something genuinely needs a lawyer, I say so.

Do I need permission to quote song lyrics?

Yes, in almost every case, and it's the single most expensive mistake in memoir. Lyrics are short, which means quoting even one line is quoting a large proportion of the work, and rights holders charge accordingly when they agree at all. Poetry is much the same.

Titles are fine, since titles aren't copyright, so you can write that the song playing was "Wichita Lineman" and describe what it did to the room. That's usually better writing anyway than reproducing the words.

What if I can't remember exactly what was said?

Nobody can, and readers know it. The convention in memoir is that dialogue is reconstructed to the best of your memory, not transcribed, and a short author's note saying so covers you completely.

What breaks trust isn't imperfect recall, it's false precision: the perfectly remembered forty-line conversation from 1974. Where you're genuinely unsure, say so on the page. "I remember it as a Tuesday, though my sister is certain it was the weekend" is more convincing than either version stated flatly.

Can you write it for me?

For a private family memoir, yes. That's a normal way to do it: recorded conversations, transcribed and shaped into chapters, with you reading and correcting every draft.

Ghostwriting a memoir intended for commercial publication is also completely normal in the trade, and the rules there are simply that it stays confidential and your name is the one on the cover.

What I won't do is write it and let you believe you wrote it. That's not a service, it's a story you'd have to keep up.

The book ghostwriting page covers how that actually runs: the interviews, the chapter plan, staged payments, and who owns what at the end.

Is my life interesting enough for a memoir?

That's the wrong question, and it stops more books than any other.

Nobody reads a memoir because the events were remarkable. They read it because the writing makes an ordinary thing legible, and because the book is about something rather than merely being an account of what happened.

A childhood in a Midlands village can hold a reader for 80,000 words. An extraordinary life told as a list of events will lose them by page thirty.

Will you tell me if it isn't working?

Yes, and this is the one people find hardest about memoir editing, because the material is the author's actual life.

A note saying a chapter isn't earning its place is never a comment on the year it describes. I keep those two things separate and I say so explicitly in the report, but you should know before you send it that the feedback will be honest.

If what you want is confirmation, I'd be taking your money under a misunderstanding.
