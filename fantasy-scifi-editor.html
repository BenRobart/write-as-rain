The specific indignities of writing at this length, in this genre

  • Somebody changed "the Vaelthi" to "the Vaelthis" throughout, and you spent a Sunday checking every instance by hand.
  • Your deliberately archaic register came back flagged as "awkward phrasing". Four hundred times.
  • Nobody would give you a number for 140,000 words. Every quote came back as "please enquire".
  • Your editor missed the magic-system contradiction in book two. A Goodreads reviewer didn't, and you're drafting book three now.
  • Your prologue was recommended for deletion by somebody who hadn't read past it.
  • You've now spent more hours maintaining your continuity spreadsheet than writing chapter thirty.

The glossary comes first

Before I edit a word of a secondary-world manuscript, I read the whole thing once and build a glossary. Every invented proper noun goes in, and each gets a proper entry rather than just a spelling.

  • Spelling and diacritics. Including whether the accent survives in dialogue, in a chapter heading, and in small caps, which is where they usually go missing.
  • Capitalisation policy. The Empire or the empire? A Binder or a binder? The answer can legitimately differ by context, and it wants deciding once rather than forty times.
  • Plurals and possessives, especially the irregular ones you chose on purpose. If the plural of Vaelthi is Vaelthi, that goes down as a decision rather than a typo.
  • Italicisation, and the point at which a term has become familiar enough to stop being italicised.
  • Who uses which word. An imperial officer and a farmer rarely use the same term for the same thing, so swapping them is a characterisation error wearing a vocabulary error's coat.

Spellcheck and autocorrect get switched off for the lot. Nothing quietly becomes an English word that already exists.

The glossary is yours to keep. It arrives as a separate document with the edit. Useful for the sequel, obviously, but also for your narrator, your cover designer, and the map artist who would otherwise spell three place names differently from the manuscript and only tell you after printing.

Magic and technology, held to their own rules

The most damaging inconsistency in speculative fiction is never a typo. It's a character doing something in chapter thirty-eight that the rules you set up in chapter three should have stopped.

Your readers are unusually rigorous about this, and they don't file it under "oversight". They file it under "the author cheated to get out of a corner", and trust takes a long time to come back from there.

So I track the constraints while I read. Costs, cooldowns, required components, distances, anything stated to be impossible. Every later breach gets flagged with a reference back to the passage that established the rule, so you can decide whether to change the scene, change the rule, or add the one line of explanation that resolves the whole thing.

Science fiction gets the same treatment. Travel times that vary for no reason, comms that work when the plot needs them and fail when it doesn't, and the ship that holds a different number of people in every act.

Timelines, maps, and the tyranny of travel

Journeys are where secondary worlds fall apart quietly. A fortnight's ride from the capital to the coast in chapter six cannot become four days in chapter twenty-two just because the plot has grown impatient.

I keep a running timeline as I go. Days elapsed, seasons, moon phases if you've committed to them, character ages, and how long each journey genuinely takes at the speeds you've established. Multi-POV books get this per thread, which is how I can tell you that two storylines due to converge have drifted about a week apart.

Exposition, and why the problem is usually placement

Nobody who picked up your book resents worldbuilding. They bought it for the worldbuilding. The problem is almost never how much there is.

So I don't cut lore. I mark the paragraphs where a reader is being handed six hundred years of history at the exact moment they urgently want to know whether someone survived a fall, and suggest where the same material might land better. Split across two later scenes. Moved into dialogue with a character who has a reason to explain it. Held back until curiosity has actually built.

All comments, all optional. I'd much rather you overruled me knowingly than followed me blindly.

Ensemble casts and POV discipline

Past four viewpoint characters, the same problems turn up with great reliability. Two POVs who narrate identically. A viewpoint that appears once in the middle of the book and is never heard from again. Head-hopping inside a scene that you didn't notice because you knew what everyone was thinking anyway. A character the reader has completely lost track of, because it's been 180 pages.

You get a POV map with the edit: who narrates which chapter, how long since each character last appeared, and where the gaps have grown long enough that a reader will need reminding who somebody is.

Length, honestly

Self-publishing? Length is a print-cost question and a pacing question. Epic fantasy readers buy doorstoppers on purpose and nobody is going to turn you down for it.

Querying agents? Different situation, and I'd rather be blunt about it. A debut much over 120,000 words is a genuine obstacle. Agents don't dislike long books; they dislike expensive risks, and a first-time author is already a risk before you add another 40,000 words of production cost to it.

I'll tell you which of those two situations you're in and what a realistic target looks like. If the book honestly needs its length, you'll get that answer too.

Fantasy & sci-fi editing questions

How much does it cost to edit a 120,000-word fantasy novel?

£710 for a copyedit. The sums: £350 covers the first 60,000 words, and the remaining 60,000 come in at £6 per 1,000, which is £360.

That price includes a full proofread as the final pass, plus the glossary and style sheet. Word count gets confirmed and the number fixed before any paid work starts, so it doesn't creep upward when the file turns out to be longer than you remembered.

Will you cut my worldbuilding?

Not on my own authority, no. Worldbuilding is the reason anybody bought the book.

What I'll do is mark the specific paragraphs where lore is arriving at a moment the reader wants something else entirely, which is nearly always mid-tension, and suggest where it might go instead. Comments, not deletions. You're free to read the note and decide I've got it wrong.

How do you handle invented names and languages?

Every invented proper noun goes into a glossary, with spelling, diacritics, capitalisation, plural, possessive, and italicisation policy all recorded. Spellcheck and autocorrect get disabled for those terms so nothing gets silently normalised into an English word.

Built an actual conlang with its own grammar? Send whatever notes you have. I don't need to speak it. I need to know which of its rules I mustn't break.

What's a magic system continuity check?

Reading your book against its own rules. Whatever limits you set up early on, whether that's a cost, a cooldown, a component, or a flat statement that something can't be done, I track them and flag every later scene that breaks them without explanation.

It matters because your readers won't file this under "small error". They'll file it under "the author cheated", and that's a considerably harder hole to climb out of.

Is my epic fantasy too long?

Depends entirely on how you're publishing it.

  • Self-publishing: largely a print-cost and pacing question. Epic fantasy readers buy long books on purpose.
  • Querying agents: a debut much over 120,000 words is a real obstacle. That isn't snobbery. A first novel is already a risk and a long one is a more expensive risk.

I'll tell you which situation you're in, and whether the book genuinely needs its length or has simply acquired it over four years.

Can you keep a trilogy consistent across all three books?

Yes. The glossary and style sheet carry forward and grow with each volume, holding names, spellings, system rules, distances, in-world dates, and a note of who knows what at the end of each book.

That last one catches the most common series error there is: a character acting on information they haven't actually been told yet, because you knew it and forgot they didn't.

Do you edit LitRPG, progression fantasy, or cultivation novels?

Yes, and they carry consistency burdens a general editor won't even be looking for. Stat blocks and system messages formatted identically every single time. Numbers that actually add up across levels. Skill names that don't drift. Progression that feels earned rather than handed over when it's convenient.

If the chapters were serialised on Royal Road or Patreon first, there's usually extra work stripping out the recap habits and repeated explanations. They're necessary over a long weekly run and read as padding in one volume.

Should I cut my prologue?

Only if it isn't earning its place. "It's a prologue" isn't a reason on its own, whatever the internet says, and the blanket advice against them has hardened into dogma.

The useful question is what yours is doing. A prologue showing a scene the reader can't get any other way tends to work. A prologue delivering historical background before anybody cares about anything tends not to. I'll tell you which one you've written and why.
