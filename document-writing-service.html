Writing · Documents

Your notes, turned into a finished document

You've written it out by hand, or typed something rough at eleven at night. It says what you mean. It just doesn't look or sound like the thing you actually need to send.

Send it exactly as it is – photographs of handwritten pages are completely fine – and it comes back as a proper document you can print, post, or attach, still saying what you meant by it.

Send it in whatever state it's in

Nothing needs typing up first. Photographs taken on your phone are fine, as long as the whole page is in frame and roughly in focus. A voice note is fine. Bullet points in the body of an email are fine.

Six sides of A4 in biro, out of order, with two paragraphs crossed out and an arrow pointing to something on the back – also fine, and far more common than you'd think.

The instinct to make it presentable before sending it to somebody is completely understandable, and it's the single most common reason these documents never get written at all. There's no version of this where you have to do the hard part first.

Two starting points, one ending

People arrive here with one of two things.

The first is raw material. Notes, a list, a timeline scrawled on the back of an envelope, or a conversation you've had in your head a hundred times and never once written down. That gets written into a document.

The second is a draft that exists and doesn't work. Usually it's too long, in the order things happened rather than the order they matter, too angry, too apologetic, or simply not the sort of thing the person receiving it will act on. That gets rebuilt rather than patched.

Both end in the same place: a finished file, formatted properly, that you can send and stop thinking about.

The sorts of things people send

  • Letters that have to work. Complaints to a council, a landlord, an insurer, an energy supplier, or a hospital. Appeals. Formal responses to something you've received.
  • Letters that are hard for other reasons. A resignation, an apology, a boundary set politely, or the letter to a family member you've been drafting in your head since March.
  • Business documents. Proposals, reports, policies, procedures, terms explained in plain English, handover notes, and the induction pack that has lived entirely in somebody's head for six years.
  • Organisation paperwork. Committee papers, a constitution, an AGM report, a funding update for members, or the newsletter for a club, a church, or a village hall.
  • Personal documents. An account of what happened, written down once so you don't have to keep retelling it. A letter supporting somebody else's case. Something for a file that matters.
  • One-offs. Instructions for a house sitter, an order of service, a family notice, a set of directions somebody will actually be able to follow.

If it's a document and it has to say something to somebody, it's probably in scope. If it isn't, I'll tell you and point you at whoever does that.

One line worth drawing: a newsletter here means a one-off, written from what you send. A newsletter that goes out every month in your own voice, built from a standing conversation, is ghostwriting, and it's priced as a retainer rather than per document.

Handwriting is genuinely fine

Reading other people's handwriting is part of the job rather than an imposition, and it very rarely holds anything up.

Two rules cover the parts that matter. I don't guess, and I don't invent. If a word is unreadable, or a sentence could plausibly go two ways, it comes back to you as a question alongside the draft, rather than as a confident sentence that might be wrong.

Dates, names, amounts, and reference numbers get checked with you explicitly, every time, because those are the ones that do real damage when they're wrong.

If you'd rather talk than write, that works too. Half an hour on the phone usually produces more usable material than an evening spent staring at a blank page, and some documents – the personal ones especially – are far easier said aloud than drafted.

It still has to sound like you

The usual worry is ending up with something stiff and official that you'd be embarrassed to put your name to. A document that reads as though a solicitor wrote it is not automatically a better document, and if it doesn't sound like you, you won't send it.

So the voice stays. What changes is the order, the length, and the clarity:

  • Before: "I am writing to express my dissatisfaction with the ongoing situation regarding my property."
    After: "The heating in my flat has not worked since 4 November. I have reported it four times."
  • Before: Everything that happened, in the order it happened, for a page and a half.
    After: What you want, in the first two lines, with the history underneath it as dates. Whoever opens this reads the top and skims the rest, and that's true whether it's a housing officer or a chief executive.
  • Before: "Sorry to be a pain, I know you're busy, it's probably my fault but…"
    After: The same request, without the apology in front of it. The politeness survives. The apology wasn't earning its place.

Firm and rude aren't the same thing, and the difference is worth money. A letter that reads as furious gets filed as a difficult customer and answered with a template. The one that gets acted on is calm, dated, specific, and impossible to misread.

What comes back

  • A Word file and a PDF. The Word file so you can change it later; the PDF so it looks the same on their screen as it did on yours.
  • Formatted properly: consistent headings, sensible spacing, page numbers on anything long, addresses set out the way letters are, and a subject line or reference where one belongs.
  • A plain text version if it's going into an online form or a portal, because those strip formatting and quietly eat anything clever.
  • Tracked changes, if you sent a draft of your own, so you can see every single alteration and reject any you don't like. Your original file comes back untouched.
  • A short list of anything I couldn't resolve: the illegible word, the date that doesn't line up, the figure you'll want to double-check before it goes.

Then revisions, until it's right. Those are included rather than counted.

What I won't do

Worth being clear about, because these are the questions that come up once work has started.

  • I won't invent facts. If the document needs a date, a figure, or an event that isn't in what you sent, you get a question, not a plausible-sounding sentence.
  • I'm not a solicitor, and none of this is legal advice. I can set out clearly what happened and what you're asking for. I can't tell you what you're entitled to, and I'll say so rather than guess. The same goes for financial, medical, and tax questions.
  • I won't write something meant to mislead the person receiving it. Putting your case at its strongest is the job. Putting a false one is not.
  • I won't talk you into a document you don't need. If what this really wants to be is a two-line email, you'll be told that, and there'll be nothing to pay.

How quickly

A letter is usually same day or next day. A longer document is normally two to three days from the point I've got everything I need.

If there's a date attached – a complaint window closing, a deadline for responses, a meeting it has to reach – put it in your first message. You'll get a straight answer about whether it's possible, including when it isn't.

Confidentiality

A good deal of this work is private, and some of it is painful. Documents are never shared, never used as samples or examples anywhere – including on this website – and are deleted on request.

Questions people ask

Can I just send photos of handwritten pages?

Yes. Photos taken on a phone are fine, as long as the whole page is in frame and roughly in focus. Nothing needs typing up first.

Mine is very rough. Is that a problem?

No, and it's the normal starting point. Bullet points, half sentences, paragraphs crossed out, and an arrow pointing at something on the back of the page are all usable material.

The instinct to tidy it up before sending is the thing that stops most of these documents ever getting written.

Can you write a complaint letter that gets results?

I can make it clear, dated, specific, and easy for the person receiving it to act on, which is most of the battle. Complaints usually fail because the reader can't find what happened or what's being asked for, not because they weren't angry enough.

And no letter can force anybody to do anything. Anyone promising otherwise is selling you something.
